Every disastrous installation I have been called in to rescue started the same way: someone quoted from a floor plan and a photograph. The floor plan was drawn before the building was renovated, and the photograph did not show the electrical panel.

A survey is an hour on site. It is the cheapest hour in the whole project, and it is the difference between a fixed price and an argument.

## Why the drawing lies

Architectural drawings describe intent. Buildings describe history. In a fit-out you are working with whatever the last three tenants did, and none of it was documented.

The specific things drawings get wrong, in my experience, in order of how much they cost to discover late: the electrical supply type, the position of the waste stack, the actual ceiling void depth, and whether the counter is where the drawing says it is. Any one of those can move an installation date by a month.

> Measure the building you are installing into, not the building someone designed. If you cannot get on site before quoting, quote with a written exclusion list rather than absorbing the risk silently.

## Power

This is the first thing I look at, because it is the item with the longest lead time when it is wrong.

- **Supply type at the panel.** Single-phase or three-phase, and the voltage. Photograph the panel schedule and the main breaker rating.
- **Spare breaker ways, and spare *capacity*.** These are different questions. A panel can have empty ways and no headroom left on the main.
- **Distance from panel to machine position.** Cable is priced by the metre and voltage drop is a function of run length. A 25 m run changes the cable size.
- **What else is on the intended circuit.** The answer must eventually be "nothing".
- **Where the isolator will live.** It has to be reachable without moving the machine.

## Water

- **Source, and where the nearest usable connection is.** Under the counter is ideal. Across the room means a chase and a floor.
- **Static pressure, measured with a gauge.** Not "it seems fine".
- **A water sample, tested properly.** Alkalinity and hardness by titration, chloride, chlorine. This drives the entire treatment specification, and treatment has lead time.
- **Pipe size and material,** and whether there is an isolation valve that actually shuts.

## Drainage

The one people leave until last and the one that most often forces a redesign.

- **Where the waste connection is, and its height.** You need fall from the machine drain to the waste. If the waste is higher than the drain tray, you are into a pump, which is a permanent maintenance liability.
- **Distance and available slope.** Long horizontal runs with minimal fall silt up with coffee grounds and milk.
- **Whether an air gap or indirect waste is required by local code.** It usually is.

## The counter

- **Depth, height, and the void below.** Machine depth plus rear service clearance plus plumbing has to fit within the counter depth. It frequently does not.
- **What is directly behind the machine position.** A wall, a window, or a customer's face changes the machine choice.
- **Whether the counter can carry the load.** A three-group machine full of water is heavy, and a grinder either side adds more.
- **Cutout feasibility.** If the counter is stone and already installed, a cutout is a specialist job with a real chance of cracking.

## Access

Measure the route the machine will actually travel, end to end, at its narrowest point.

- Door widths, including the door stop and any handle that does not come off.
- Stair widths, turns, and headroom on the turn.
- Lift internal dimensions **and** its rated load.
- Whether the delivery vehicle can get within a sensible carry distance.
- Floor protection requirements — new floors and heavy castors are a bad combination.

A three-group machine that will not fit through the door is a story every technician has. It is always avoidable and always expensive.

## Heat and ventilation

Espresso machines, grinders and undercounter refrigeration all reject heat into the room. Note the existing HVAC provision, whether there is any mechanical extract behind the bar, and where the fridge condensers will breathe. If there is a roaster in the same space, that is a separate conversation entirely.

## The survey sheet

I take the same measurements in the same order every time, because the one you skip is the one that bites. Minimum viable sheet:

| Area | Record |
| --- | --- |
| Power | Phase, voltage, main rating, spare ways, spare capacity, run length, isolator position |
| Water | Connection position, static pressure, alkalinity, hardness, chloride, chlorine, pipe size |
| Waste | Position, height relative to machine, run length, available fall, code requirement |
| Counter | Depth, height, void, load capacity, material, rear clearance |
| Access | Narrowest point on the route, lift dimensions and load, delivery position |
| Environment | HVAC provision, extract, condenser clearance, ambient temperature |
| Photos | Panel and schedule, water connection, waste, counter void, full access route |

Photograph everything. You will be asked a question in three weeks that you did not think to write down, and the photo will answer it.

## What the survey is really for

Two things. It lets you price the work accurately, which protects the client as much as it protects you. And it lets you tell the client *now* about the thing that is going to be a problem, while it is still a design decision rather than a change order.

The conversation where you explain that the waste is 300 mm above the machine drain goes well before the machine is ordered. It goes very badly on installation day.
