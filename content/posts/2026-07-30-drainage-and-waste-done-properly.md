Drainage gets designed last, installed worst, and then generates complaints for the life of the bar. It is also the part most likely to fail an inspection, because the requirement is a code matter and inspectors know exactly where to look.

The whole problem is that a coffee bar drain carries three things that no ordinary waste pipe is designed around: abrasive grounds that settle, fats and proteins from milk that build biofilm, and near-boiling water that arrives in slugs.

## Fall, or you have a storage tank

Waste needs continuous fall from the machine drain box to the connection. Aim for around 2% — roughly 1:50, or a quarter-inch per foot — and never accept a flat run "because it will be fine".

A flat or back-falling section does not simply drain slowly. It becomes a settling chamber. Grounds drop out of suspension in the still water, milk residue binds them, and within a couple of months you have a solid plug that no amount of flushing clears.

Two related failures I see constantly:

- **Unsupported pipe that sags.** A perfectly specified 2% run with one unsupported span develops a belly, and the belly is a trap. Support the pipe properly along its whole length.
- **The waste is higher than the machine drain.** Discovered on installation day, always. This forces a pump, and a pump behind a coffee bar is a permanent maintenance item.

> If the survey shows the waste connection above the machine drain height, raise it as a design problem immediately. Solving it with a pump is a decision the client should make knowingly, not a detail you absorb.

## The air gap is not optional

Local codes differ in wording but agree in principle: equipment that touches potable water must not be directly connected to the sewer. A blockage downstream must never be able to push waste water back into the machine's drain tray.

That means an **indirect waste** — the machine's drain line terminates above a floor sink, trapped gully or standpipe with a visible air gap, typically at least twice the pipe diameter. It stays open to atmosphere.

Directly gluing the machine drain into the waste stack is quick, tidy, and the first thing a health inspector will fail. It also means every sewer smell in the building vents into your drain tray.

## Use the right pipe

The flexible corrugated hose that arrives coiled in the box is for getting the machine tested at the factory. It is not an installation material.

Corrugations are hundreds of small annular traps. Grounds lodge in every one, milk fat coats them, and the hose becomes a biofilm reactor that cannot be cleaned by flushing. Cut one open after a year in service and you will not specify one again.

Use **smooth-bore pipe**, sized to code — 40 mm or 1½" is typical for a machine drain — with swept fittings rather than tight elbows, and as few direction changes as you can manage.

## Design for cleaning, because it will need cleaning

This is the part that separates an install that ages well from one that generates callouts.

- **Accessible cleanout or a demountable joint** at the machine end, reachable without moving the machine or dismantling the counter.
- **A drain box with a removable strainer** under the counter, so grounds are caught somewhere a human can empty them.
- **Never plumb a knock box to the drain.** Whatever the fitting promises, a knock chute delivers compacted pucks into a pipe. It blocks. Knock boxes go to a bin.
- **Keep the run short.** Every metre is more surface for biofilm and more distance for solids to settle.

## The smell is milk

When a bar develops that sour, faintly cheesy smell that nobody can locate, the instinct is to blame coffee. It is almost never coffee.

Milk proteins and fats coat the inside of the pipe and feed a bacterial biofilm. Steam wand purges, dumped milk and rinse water all contribute, and the biofilm establishes fastest in exactly the low-flow, warm, poorly-falling sections described above.

What works:

- **Enzymatic or bio-active drain cleaner, weekly.** It digests fat and protein, which is the actual deposit. Left in overnight so it has dwell time.
- **Volume flushes of hot water** at close, to move solids along before they settle.
- **Not bleach.** It smells like action and does very little to fats, and repeated use is unkind to seals and stainless.

Dry traps are the other cause. A trap that is not used for a week loses its water seal and vents sewer gas straight into the room. If a floor sink only sees occasional use, someone needs to run water into it deliberately.

## Pumped drains, if you must

Sometimes the building leaves no option. If a pump is genuinely necessary:

- Specify one rated for waste with solids, not a condensate pump.
- Put it where it can be accessed and serviced without demolition.
- Fit a strainer upstream and make emptying it part of the weekly routine.
- Tell the client plainly that it is a consumable with a service interval, and that it will eventually fail. Coffee grounds are abrasive.

## Commissioning checks

1. Pour a full jug of water into the drain tray and watch it clear. Slow is a fault, not a characteristic.
2. Confirm the air gap is visible and to code, and photograph it for the handover file.
3. Check every support along the run, and sight down the pipe for bellies.
4. Run the steam wand purge and a group flush together to confirm the run copes with simultaneous flow.
5. Verify traps hold water and show the client which ones need occasional filling.
6. Put the enzymatic clean on the weekly cleaning schedule with a named owner.

Nobody notices good drainage. That is the whole objective — the alternative is a bar that smells and a technician who visits every few months to rod a pipe that should never have blocked.
