Ask the HVAC engineer on a café fit-out what equipment load they allowed for behind the bar. The usual answer is a pause, followed by a question about how much there is.

Cooling loads get calculated from floor area, glazing, occupancy and lighting. Kitchen equipment gets a schedule because commercial kitchens have long-established design practice. The coffee bar falls between the two, and its equipment routinely goes in as a round-number guess or not at all.

## Where the heat comes from

Almost every watt an espresso machine draws ends up as heat in the room. A small amount leaves in the drinks. The rest radiates off the boiler, the group heads and the bodywork, or goes down the drain as hot water.

For planning purposes, over a busy service:

| Equipment | Sensible heat to room |
| --- | --- |
| Three-group machine, in service | 1.5–2.5 kW average, higher on warm-up |
| Grinders | 0.1–0.3 kW average at normal duty |
| Undercounter fridge, per unit | 0.3–0.6 kW, rising as ambient rises |
| Batch brewer / hot water tap | 0.5–1.5 kW average |

A three-group bar with two grinders, two undercounter fridges and a brewer sits somewhere around 3–5 kW of continuous sensible heat during service. That is a couple of domestic fan heaters running behind the counter all day, in a space that was probably sized without them.

Refrigeration deserves particular attention because it is counter-intuitive: a fridge is a heat *pump*, not a heat sink. Everything it removes from the cabinet, plus the compressor's own work, is rejected into the room. Adding an undercounter fridge to a hot bar makes the bar hotter.

## What the heat actually costs you

### Staff

This is the one that matters most and gets discussed least. Baristas work in a fixed two-metre space directly in front of the largest heat source in the building. Once ambient behind the bar reaches the low thirties Celsius, performance degrades — people get slower, less precise, and less inclined to do the discretionary cleaning that keeps equipment alive.

### Refrigeration reliability

Compressors are rated at an ambient temperature. Run them consistently above it and you get shorter cycles, higher head pressure, and a compressor that fails in year three instead of year eight. Undercounter units squeezed into an unventilated cabinet next to an espresso machine are the classic case.

### Machine electronics

Control boards, SSRs and pumps in a sealed cabinet with a hot boiler above them run at whatever the cabinet reaches. Electrolytic capacitors age roughly twice as fast for every 10 °C rise. A machine in a well-ventilated position and an identical machine boxed into a tight cutout have genuinely different service lives.

### Grind consistency

Burrs heat up in service — from motor heat, from the work of grinding, and from the ambient air the grinder is breathing. Hot burrs change grind particle distribution, and the effect is enough that a bar can need a different setting at 4 p.m. than at 8 a.m. Some of that is unavoidable; a lot of it is a grinder sitting in a pocket of hot air.

> If baristas are chasing the grinder all day and blaming the beans, measure the air temperature at the grinder intake before you change anything else.

## Getting it designed properly

- **Give the HVAC engineer a real equipment schedule.** Nameplate kW, expected duty factor, and position on the plan. Ask them to confirm in writing that the bar equipment is in the cooling load. This one email prevents most of the problem.
- **Ask for dedicated extract behind the bar.** General room cooling delivered from the middle of the ceiling does very little for a barista standing in a hot pocket. Local extract over the machine is far more effective per watt.
- **Do not treat the counter void as a sealed box.** It contains a pump, a motor, electronics and often refrigeration. It needs a deliberate air path in and out.

## Condensers need clearance, and it is a specification

Every refrigeration unit has a manufacturer's minimum clearance for its condenser airflow. Cabinetmakers do not read those documents, and the result is a fridge in a snug custom recess with no path for air.

Rules I hold to:

- Honour the manufacturer's stated clearance, front-breathing units included.
- Never place a condenser intake where it can draw the espresso machine's exhaust or a fridge's own discharge.
- Make the condenser coil reachable for cleaning. A dusty coil behind a fixed panel is a guaranteed failure, and coffee bars are dusty places — grinder chaff gets everywhere.
- On a tight bar, consider remote condensing. It is more capital and it moves the heat and the noise out of the room entirely.

## Commissioning measurements worth taking

Take these on a busy day, not on a quiet install afternoon:

1. Air temperature at barista head height behind the bar, at the start and peak of service.
2. Air temperature at the grinder intake.
3. Temperature inside the counter void near the machine's electronics.
4. Fridge cabinet temperature under load, plus the ambient at its condenser intake.
5. Note them all in the handover file as a baseline.

That baseline is what lets you answer "has it always been like this?" two years later, which is otherwise unanswerable.

## The argument that works with clients

Heat mitigation is unglamorous and it competes for budget against visible things. The version of the argument that lands is not about comfort — it is that ambient heat is quietly setting the replacement interval on every piece of equipment behind the counter, including the two most expensive items.

Extract behind the bar costs a fraction of one compressor and one control board. Framed that way, it usually survives the value-engineering meeting.
