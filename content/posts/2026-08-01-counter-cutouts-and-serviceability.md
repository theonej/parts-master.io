Cabinetmakers build beautiful counters. They build them to a drawing, in a workshop, months before anyone thinks about how a pump gets replaced.

By the time I am on site, the counter is stone, installed, and the only way to reach the inlet solenoid is to take the machine out through the front. Nobody made a bad decision — the decision just got made by someone who was never going to service the equipment.

## Depth is where it goes wrong

The arithmetic is simple and routinely skipped. Counter depth has to accommodate machine depth, plus rear clearance, plus the plumbing and electrical behind it.

A three-group machine is often 580–620 mm deep. A standard 600 mm counter therefore has *nothing* left for the hoses, the union, the isolator, or the space a hand needs to reach a fitting. Add 100–150 mm behind the machine as a minimum, and specify the counter from that total rather than from a catalogue standard.

If the machine will sit against a wall, remember that the drain hose leaves at the back and needs a bend radius that does not kink.

> Get the manufacturer's installation drawing — not the brochure — before the counter is fabricated. The brochure gives you the body dimensions. The installation drawing gives you the service clearances, and those are the numbers the cabinetmaker actually needs.

## Cutouts to specify

Give the cabinetmaker a marked drawing, not a description. What has to pass through the counter:

- **Water inlet**, with room for the union and an isolation valve you can turn.
- **Drain**, straight down into the drain box, with no lateral kink.
- **Electrical**, in its own opening, kept away from water penetrations.
- **The drain box position**, so the machine's drip tray outlet lands over it.

Put the cutouts slightly oversized and cover the gap with an escutcheon. A tight cutout means the machine cannot be lifted straight up without disconnecting everything, which turns a fifteen-minute job into an afternoon.

## The machine has to come out

This is the test I apply to every counter design: can two people lift the machine off the counter without dismantling anything?

If the answer is no, then every future repair that needs rear or underside access becomes a major operation, and the client gets quoted accordingly for the rest of the machine's life.

What makes it possible:

- **Service loops on all connections.** Enough slack in the braided inlet hose, the drain and the flex to slide the machine forward 200 mm and still have it connected. Factory hoses are usually short — specify longer ones at order time.
- **Unions, not fixed joints.** Everything that connects to the machine should disconnect with a spanner, at a fitting you can reach.
- **No sealant between machine and counter.** Do not silicone the body down. It happens surprisingly often, in pursuit of a clean line, and it means the machine has to be cut free.
- **Feet, not a recess.** A machine dropped into a snug routed recess cannot be slid forward at all.

## The void below

Everything that needs regular attention lives under the counter: the water filter, the pump, the drain box, the isolation valves.

- **An access door big enough for shoulders,** positioned in front of the equipment rather than around a corner from it.
- **Filter cartridge clearance.** Measure the cartridge's swing or drop distance and confirm it clears. A filter head mounted with 50 mm below it needs the whole assembly removed to change a cartridge, so it will not get changed.
- **A removable strainer on the drain box** that someone can lift out and empty.
- **Light and a dry floor.** Nobody diagnoses a leak lying in a dark cupboard.
- **Ventilation.** The void contains a motor and electronics. It needs an air path.

## Working height, which is not counter height

Counters get built to 900–950 mm because that is standard. But the barista does not work at counter height — they work at the height of the drip tray and the group head.

Add a tall traditional machine to a standard counter and the group is high enough that locking in a portafilter means lifting at an awkward shoulder angle, hundreds of times a shift. Add a low-profile machine and the same counter works well.

So choose the machine and the counter height together. If the machine is already chosen and it is tall, drop the counter. Machine height and counter height are one decision, and it is an ergonomic one.

## Stone, and other unforgiving materials

Stone and solid-surface counters need their cutouts fabricated with the template, off site. A cutout attempted afterwards on installed stone carries a genuine risk of cracking, and the repair is a new counter.

That means the plumbing and electrical positions have to be finalised earlier than anyone wants to finalise them. Push for it anyway: a template signed off two weeks late is inconvenient, and a cracked counter is a five-figure conversation.

Leave generous internal radii on any cutout corner. Sharp internal corners are where stone starts a crack.

## Handover check

1. Lift the machine forward 200 mm with everything connected. If it will not move, say so in writing before you leave.
2. Change the filter cartridge once, for real, with the machine in place.
3. Open and close the access door and reach every valve from a normal crouch.
4. Confirm the drip tray outlet actually lands in the drain box, with water.
5. Photograph the void with everything labelled, for the file.

None of this shows in a photograph of the finished bar. It is the entire difference between a machine that gets maintained and one that gets neglected because maintaining it is a nuisance.
