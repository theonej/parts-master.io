A busy barista repeats one movement sequence several hundred times a shift: dose, tamp, lock in, start, steam, pour, knock out, rinse. Every unnecessary step or turn in that sequence gets multiplied by that number.

Bar layout is usually settled on aesthetic and plumbing grounds, then the humans adapt. It is worth inverting: design the movement sequence first, then position the equipment that serves it.

## The working triangle

Borrowed from kitchen design and more relevant here, because the distances are shorter and the repetitions higher. Three points:

- **The grinder**, where the dose starts.
- **The group**, where it is brewed.
- **The steam wand and milk**, where the drink is finished.

All three should be reachable without the barista moving their feet. If any leg of the triangle requires a step, that step happens hundreds of times a day.

The most common violation is a grinder placed at the far end of the counter because that is where it fitted. The barista then walks two paces per drink, which over a shift is a meaningful distance and, more importantly, breaks the rhythm that makes a busy bar work.

> Count the steps required to make one latte on your drawing. On a well-arranged bar the answer is zero. If it is more than one, move something.

## Distances that work

- **Grinder to group: within arm's reach**, ideally with the knock box between them so the spent puck is dispatched on the way past.
- **Steam wand to milk fridge: same side, no turn.** Reaching across the body for a milk jug is the most common source of shoulder complaints behind a bar.
- **Cups within reach without turning away from the customer.** Cup storage behind the barista means turning your back, which costs both time and the interaction.
- **Behind-counter width: 1.2 m minimum for one person, 1.5 m if two people will pass.** Below 1.2 m, two baristas physically cannot work simultaneously, whatever the plan says.

## Heights, and the mistake that causes injuries

The critical height is not the counter. It is the **group head**, because that is where the repeated lifting and twisting happens.

Lock-in is a movement with the forearm raised and the wrist rotating under load. Do it at chest height and it is a comfortable motion. Do it at shoulder height, which is what a tall traditional machine on a 950 mm counter produces, and you have a repetitive strain injury waiting on a schedule.

The tamping surface matters for the same reason. Tamping needs a downward press with the forearm roughly horizontal, so a surface that is too high turns it into a shoulder movement.

Practically: choose the machine and set the counter height as one decision. A low-profile machine tolerates a standard counter. A tall machine wants the counter dropped, often to 850–880 mm.

## Traffic, and who crosses whom

Draw three paths on the plan and check they do not conflict:

1. **The barista's path** — should be almost stationary.
2. **The customer's path** — order point to collection point, moving in one direction.
3. **The runner or restocker's path** — into the bar for milk, cups and beans.

The failure mode is a restocker needing to reach the milk fridge through the space the barista occupies during service. It works at 9 a.m. and falls apart at the peak. Put restock access at the end of the run, reachable from outside the working position.

Customers collecting drinks should not have to pass the espresso machine — there is hot water and steam there, and it forces the barista to stop working while someone squeezes past.

## Details that get missed

- **Handedness.** Most bars are laid out right-handed by default. Ask, and if the team is mixed, keep the triangle symmetrical rather than optimised for one hand.
- **Knock box position.** It should be where the hand naturally arrives after unlocking the portafilter — not across the body, and not somewhere that requires a turn.
- **A landing surface next to the group.** Somewhere to put a jug or a portafilter down. Bars designed to look minimal frequently have nowhere to put anything, and baristas end up balancing things on the drip tray.
- **Cable and hose routes out of the working zone.** Nothing should be draped where an elbow catches it.
- **Bin access without a turn.** Same logic as the knock box.
- **Floor.** Anti-fatigue matting where the barista stands, and a surface that does not become lethal when milk is spilt.

## Mock it up

The single most useful hour in bar design costs nothing: tape the footprint on the floor, put cardboard boxes at the real dimensions of the machine and grinders, and have a barista walk through making twenty imaginary drinks.

Problems that are invisible on a drawing become obvious immediately — the reach that is 50 mm too far, the door that opens into the working position, the fridge that cannot be opened while standing at the wand. Fixing them at that point costs a pencil line. Fixing them afterwards costs a counter.

## Why this is a technician's problem too

An awkward bar does not only tire staff. It changes maintenance behaviour, which is what I see downstream.

If the knock box is badly placed, pucks get knocked into the drain. If the group is at an awkward height, backflushing gets rushed. If reaching the grinder means moving, retention gets purged less often. If the cleaning cupboard is across the room, the daily routine gets shortened.

Good ergonomics is not only about the barista's shoulder. It is one of the better predictors of whether the equipment gets looked after.
