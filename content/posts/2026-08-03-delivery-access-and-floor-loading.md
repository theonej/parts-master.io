Every technician has the story: a three-group machine, a doorway, and a gap of fifteen millimetres. The machine goes back on the van, the opening is delayed, and everyone involved learns something they could have learned with a tape measure.

Access planning is not glamorous and takes twenty minutes. Skipping it is the most avoidable failure in this trade.

## Weights, roughly

Dry weights, before water:

| Machine | Typical dry weight |
| --- | --- |
| Two group | 60–80 kg |
| Three group | 85–115 kg |
| Four group | 120–150 kg |
| On-demand grinder | 12–25 kg |
| Large-burr / bulk grinder | 25–40 kg |

Add crating, which is often 15–25 kg and makes the package considerably larger than the machine. Then add water: boilers hold real volume, and a filled three-group machine is heavier than the number above by a useful margin.

Two important consequences. Filled machines should not be lifted — drain the boilers before moving an installed machine. And crated dimensions, not machine dimensions, are what has to fit through the building.

## Measure the route, not the destination

Walk the path the machine will travel, from the kerb to the counter, and measure the narrowest point of every stage.

- **Door widths measured between the stops,** with the door open. Note the handle — some do not come off, and they cost 30 mm.
- **Corridor turns.** A long crate cannot pivot in a narrow corridor. The diagonal is what matters, not the width.
- **Stair width, headroom, and the turn.** A half-landing turn is where long items stop. Headroom on the turn is the dimension people forget.
- **Lift internal dimensions *and* rated load.** Both. A lift big enough and rated for 100 kg does not take a 115 kg machine plus two people.
- **Thresholds and level changes,** which stop wheeled equipment.

Measure with the crate dimensions in hand. If the crated machine will not make it, decide *in advance* whether it can be uncrated at the kerb and carried in bare — usually yes, but it changes the handling risk and it needs to be a decision rather than a surprise.

> Photograph and measure the narrowest point, then send both to whoever is confirming the order. "It will be fine" is not a dimension.

## Getting it there

- **Delivery vehicle access, kerb space and permits.** In city centres this is often the binding constraint, and permits take days.
- **Carry distance from the vehicle.** A 40 m carry needs different resourcing than a 4 m one.
- **Tail lift or not.** A 115 kg crate coming off a van without a tail lift needs equipment, not enthusiasm.
- **Who is actually lifting.** Two people and a 115 kg machine up a stair turn is beyond safe manual handling. Use a sack truck rated for the load, stair-climbing equipment, or more people.
- **Floor protection.** New timber, tile and resin floors do not survive a loaded sack truck. Board the route.

## Floor loading, which is occasionally serious

On a ground-floor slab this is a non-issue. Elsewhere it deserves a moment's thought, because the load is concentrated.

A machine's weight sits on four small feet. A 115 kg machine plus water on four 30 mm feet is a significant point load, and it is sitting on a counter, which is sitting on a floor.

Ask the question in two places:

- **The counter.** Can the carcass and its supports carry the machine, two grinders and someone leaning on it? Custom joinery is not always built with a load case in mind.
- **The floor,** if it is not a ground slab. Timber floors, mezzanines and upper storeys in converted buildings all have limits. Bars are also concentrated loads generally — machine, grinders, refrigeration, water, and a queue of people in a small area.

If the bar is on a mezzanine or a timber structure, get a structural opinion in writing. It is a short consultation and it removes the one risk on this list that is a safety matter rather than an inconvenience.

## Taking delivery

The handling of the delivery itself is where warranty positions are won and lost.

1. **Inspect before signing.** Look at the crate for impact damage, and look for a tilt or shock indicator if one is fitted. Once you sign a clean delivery note, transit damage becomes your problem.
2. **Note any damage on the carrier's paperwork**, specifically, before signing. Photograph the crate from all sides, including the pallet.
3. **Uncrate and inspect the machine itself** on the same day, even if installation is next week. Concealed damage has a reporting window and it is short.
4. **Check the contents against the packing list.** Portafilters, baskets, tamper, hoses, unions, documentation. Missing accessories are easier to claim on day one.
5. **Keep the crate** until the machine is commissioned and accepted. If it has to go back, it has to go back packaged.

## Store it properly if it is early

Machines frequently arrive before the counter is ready. Store the machine somewhere dry, above freezing, off the floor, and not in the middle of an active building site where a scaffold pole can land on it.

Freezing matters more than people think. A machine that has been factory-tested has water in it, and a hard frost in an unheated store can crack fittings that will not reveal themselves until the day you pressurise the boiler.
