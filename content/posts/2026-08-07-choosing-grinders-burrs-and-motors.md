If a client has a fixed budget and asks where to spend the marginal thousand, the answer is the grinder. Every time.

An espresso machine's job is to deliver water at a stable temperature and pressure. A good one does that, and a better one does it slightly more consistently. The grinder determines particle size distribution, which sets extraction, which is most of what is in the cup. Yet grinders are routinely treated as accessories to the machine purchase and specified last, from what is left.

## Flat or conical

Both make excellent espresso. The differences are real but smaller than the arguments about them.

**Flat burrs.** Two parallel discs. Generally a tighter particle distribution, which many people taste as more clarity and separation. They retain more grounds in the chamber, run hotter under sustained use, and need the two discs to be properly parallel — alignment matters more here than on any other component.

**Conical burrs.** A cone inside a ring. Gravity assists the flow, so retention is typically lower and they run cooler. Often more forgiving of small dialling errors, and generally a longer service life at the same diameter.

For a busy commercial bar, conical burrs' lower retention and cooler running are practical advantages. For a bar built around light roasts and single origins, flat burrs' clarity is usually what people are after. Neither answer is wrong.

## Diameter is the specification that matters

More than the type. A larger burr set does less work per particle, generates less heat, grinds a dose faster, and lasts longer before wear shows.

| Burr diameter | Realistic role |
| --- | --- |
| 58–64 mm flat | Light commercial, secondary or decaf grinder |
| 71–75 mm flat | Solid main espresso grinder |
| 83–98 mm flat | High volume, or where cool running matters |
| 58–68 mm conical | Light to moderate commercial |
| 71–85 mm conical | Main espresso grinder, high volume |

Undersizing shows up as heat. A small burr set on a busy bar climbs in temperature through the peak, the grind drifts, and the barista chases it all morning. Buying diameter is buying thermal stability.

## Motor, and the specification nobody reads

Two things to ask about, neither of which appears prominently in brochures.

**Duty rating.** An espresso grinder on a busy bar runs hundreds of short cycles an hour. Motors that are not rated for that develop heat in the windings, and either the thermal cutout intervenes mid-rush or the motor's life shortens quietly. Ask for a continuous or high-duty rating explicitly.

**Speed.** Lower RPM generally means cooler running and fewer fines; higher RPM means faster dosing. Geared, low-speed grinders trade a little dose time for meaningfully less heat, which on a hot bar is a good trade.

The other motor question is torque at start under load. A grinder that stalls on a full hopper of dense beans, or that struggles when started with grounds in the chamber, is undersized for the job.

## Dosing

**Timed on-demand** is the commercial standard. A timer runs the motor for a set duration. Simple and reliable — but it doses by *time*, so the delivered mass moves with bean density, burr wear, temperature and how full the hopper is. It needs checking against scales through the day, and on most bars it does not get checked.

**Grind-by-weight** puts a load cell under the dosing point and stops at a target mass. This removes the single largest source of dose variance on a bar, which is genuinely valuable. The costs are a higher price, a slightly longer cycle, and a load cell — a precision component living in a wet, vibrating, coffee-dusty place. On high-volume sites with mixed-ability staff it earns its keep. On a small skilled bar with scales on the counter, it is optional.

> Whatever the dosing method, the bar needs scales and someone checking dose against target at least at open and after any grind change. Timed grinders drift. That is not a fault, it is how they work.

## How many grinders

Driven by the menu, not the budget.

- **One per espresso coffee served.** Switching a grinder between two coffees means the first several grams of every change are the previous coffee. It defeats the point of offering two.
- **A dedicated decaf grinder, always.** Cross-contamination is a real problem — a customer avoiding caffeine for medical reasons is relying on you. Sharing a grinder for decaf is not acceptable practice, however carefully it is purged.
- **A separate filter grinder** if the bar serves batch or pour-over. Espresso and filter grind settings are far apart, and moving a grinder between them repeatedly is slow and imprecise.

A realistic specialty bar therefore needs three grinders — house espresso, guest or single origin, and decaf — plus a filter grinder if filter is on the menu. Clients are consistently surprised by this, and it is better raised during specification than discovered during the first month.

## Serviceability

The questions to ask before ordering:

- **How does a burr change go?** Some designs are fifteen minutes with a screwdriver. Others need the grinder stripped.
- **Is alignment adjustable?** Shimmable burr carriers are a real advantage on flat-burr grinders.
- **Are burrs available as spares, at what price, and from whom?** Burrs are a consumable, not a lifetime part.
- **Is there a documented dose-adjustment and calibration procedure**, or is it folklore?
- **Noise.** Grinders are the loudest thing on the bar. On a small room it is worth asking.

## Where the money goes

Given a fixed budget on a two-group bar, the allocation I argue for:

1. Water treatment, specified from a real test.
2. Grinders — proper diameter, proper duty rating, one per coffee.
3. The espresso machine, right-sized rather than impressive.
4. Everything else.

That ordering makes some clients uncomfortable, because the machine is the visible object and the grinders are not. But the machine is a water heater with good manners. The grinder is where the coffee is decided.
