Grinder placement gets settled by whatever gap is left after the espresso machine and the till. It deserves more than that, because three separate problems are decided by it: retention, access, and heat.

## Retention, and what it actually costs

Retention is the coffee left inside the grinder — in the burr chamber, the chute, and the exit path — after a dose is delivered. What comes out with your next dose is partly the coffee you just ground and partly what was sitting there.

On a commercial on-demand grinder that is typically a few grams. Whether it matters depends entirely on how the grinder is used:

- **One coffee, continuous service.** Barely matters. The retained coffee is the same coffee, ground minutes ago.
- **First dose of the day.** Matters. That coffee has sat in a warm chamber overnight, exposed to air. Purge and discard it — the whole retained volume, not a token two seconds.
- **After changing coffee.** Matters enormously. Purge properly and discard, or the first several doses of the new coffee are a blend nobody ordered.
- **Quiet periods.** Matters more than people think. A grinder that last ran forty minutes ago is holding forty-minute-old grounds. On a bar with intermittent trade, a short purge before the dose is worth the gram.

None of this is exotic. It is a routine, and the routine only survives if the grinder is somewhere convenient with a knock chute or waste point next to it. Put the grinder where purging is awkward and purging stops happening within a fortnight.

> Write the purge routine into the opening checklist with a number in it — "discard first 15 g" — not "purge the grinder". Vague instructions decay fastest.

## Hopper access is a real constraint

The dimension that gets missed: total height with the hopper on, *plus* the vertical clearance needed to lift the hopper off.

Bars get built with an overhead shelf or an extractor at a height that clears the grinder but not the hopper removal. The result is a grinder whose hopper cannot be taken off for cleaning or coffee changes without moving the whole grinder.

Check three things against the drawing before joinery is built:

- Grinder height with hopper fitted.
- Additional clearance to lift the hopper clear of its seat.
- Whether a full bag of beans can physically be tipped in at that height, by a person of average height, without a step.

That last one determines whether hoppers get topped up little and often or filled to the brim once, which affects the grind.

## Bean weight changes the grind

The mass of beans in the hopper presses down on the burrs. A full hopper feeds slightly differently from an almost-empty one, and on some grinders the difference is enough to shift dose and grind noticeably.

Two practical consequences. Keep hopper level *consistent* rather than maximal — many bars work to a marked fill line and top up on a schedule. And if the bar dials in with a full hopper at 7 a.m., expect to check again once it has run down.

Single-dosing avoids the problem entirely, but it is slow and not realistic at volume.

## Heat, and where not to put it

Everything in the earlier heat-load post applies here specifically. Grinders should not be:

- Wedged against the espresso machine body, which is the hottest object on the counter.
- Sitting where a fridge condenser discharges into their air intake.
- In an unventilated alcove.

Burr temperature affects particle distribution, so a grinder breathing hot air produces a grind that drifts as the day heats up. Baristas experience this as "the grinder won't hold a setting" and adjust repeatedly, which masks the cause. Give the grinder cool air and half the drift disappears.

## Workflow position

From the ergonomics post: grinder, group and steam should form a triangle the barista can serve without moving their feet. Specifically for the grinder:

- **Within arm's reach of the group**, on the side the barista naturally works.
- **Knock box between grinder and group,** so the spent puck is dealt with on the path back.
- **Scales space immediately at the dosing point.** If there is nowhere to put scales, dose checking will not happen. This is a 150 mm of counter decision that determines whether the bar can verify anything.
- **Decaf grinder distinctly separated and clearly labelled.** Adjacent identical grinders with different coffees in them produce mistakes during a rush. Physical separation and labelling both help.
- **Filter grinder out of the espresso workflow entirely**, near the brew station.

## Chaff, static and cleaning

Grinders throw fine dust and chaff continuously, and static distributes it further. Within a month it is inside everything nearby.

Design for it:

- Leave access **around and behind** the grinder for cleaning, not just in front.
- Avoid placing open cup storage or exposed food directly beside a grinder.
- Keep electronics and open sockets out of the immediate area.
- Remember the espresso machine's ventilation intakes are drawing this dust in — another reason not to put the two hard against each other.

## Vibration and noise

A heavy grinder with a big motor on a hollow counter turns the whole counter into a speaker. On a small room this is genuinely unpleasant.

Check the grinder's feet are intact and doing their job, make sure the counter under it is solid rather than an unsupported span, and consider an isolating mat if the counter resonates. Also confirm the grinder is not touching the espresso machine body — direct contact transmits vibration into the machine's panels and doubles the noise.

## Commissioning checks

1. Remove and refit the hopper in position, in the real clearance.
2. Tip in a full bag of beans, standing normally.
3. Dose onto scales ten times and record the spread. That number is the bar's baseline dose consistency.
4. Measure air temperature at the grinder intake during service.
5. Run the purge routine as written and confirm it is quick enough that someone will actually do it.
6. Grind for thirty seconds continuously and check the motor housing temperature and that no thermal cutout intervenes.
