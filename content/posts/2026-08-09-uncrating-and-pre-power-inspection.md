The temptation on installation day is to get the machine on the counter, connected, and hot — because that is when it starts looking like progress. The inspection between those two states feels like a delay.

It is the highest-value twenty minutes in the whole installation. Two of the checks below prevent immediate, expensive, entirely self-inflicted damage.

## Before the crate comes off

Photograph the crate on all four sides and the pallet. Look for impact damage, crushing, and any shock or tilt indicator. If anything is marked, note it on the carrier's paperwork *before* signing.

This is a warranty position, not paperwork. Concealed transit damage has a short reporting window, and a clean signed delivery note makes it your problem.

## Voltage and element configuration: check this first

The one that matters most. Confirm the machine's data plate voltage and phase against what is actually at the site, measured.

Machines are frequently built to order for a supply configuration, and distributors occasionally ship the wrong one. Energising a machine wired for 400 V three-phase on a 230 V single-phase supply gives you a machine that will not heat properly. The reverse — energising a 230 V element set on 400 V — destroys elements immediately and can take the control gear with them.

> Measure the supply, read the data plate, and confirm they agree **before** anything is switched on. If they do not, stop. This is not a site fix.

Photograph the data plate while you are there — serial numbers, model, build date. You will need them for warranty registration and for every parts order for the next decade.

## Transit packing that lives inside the machine

Machines ship with internal protection that has to come out, and it is not always obvious or documented.

- Foam blocks and cardboard packed around boilers and behind panels.
- Cable ties restraining relays, contactors or pump assemblies.
- Shipping bolts or brackets securing the boiler or pump to the frame.
- Protective film on internal panels.
- Occasionally, desiccant sachets in the electrical bay.

Take the panels off and look. A foam block against a boiler is a fire risk on first heat-up, and a shipping bracket left on a pump mount transmits vibration into the frame for the rest of the machine's life.

## Electrical terminals

Transit vibration loosens screw terminals. This is normal, expected, and the reason a competent commissioning includes a terminal check.

Work through the electrical bay and check the tightness of element terminals, contactor connections, the incoming terminal block and the earth bonding. A loose element terminal is a high-current joint with resistance in it, which means heat, which means a burnt terminal block and eventually a fire.

While the panels are off, confirm earth continuity to the body and to the boiler, and record the reading.

## Plumbing and mechanical

- **Every fitting checked for tightness**, particularly on the pump and inlet side. Same reason as the electrical terminals.
- **Boiler and copper inspected** for dents, kinks or bright marks from impact. A dented boiler is a warranty claim on day one, not a problem to discover at pressure.
- **The pressure relief valve free**, not seized, and not painted over. Lift it by hand if the design allows.
- **Rotary pump coupling** aligned and turning freely by hand.
- **Group heads:** gaskets seated, dispersion screens present and flat, screens' retaining screws present.
- **Steam and hot water valves** opening and closing smoothly through their range.

## Never energise a dry boiler

Elements are designed to be surrounded by water. Switch on a dry boiler and the element burns out in seconds — sometimes fast enough that the safety devices do not save it.

The autofill system is supposed to prevent this, but on a first fill you are trusting a level probe that has never been tested on this machine on this site. Do not trust it yet.

So: **fill first, verify water is genuinely in the boiler, and only then energise the heating.** On machines with a manual fill valve, use it. On autofill machines, let the fill cycle complete and confirm at the sight glass or by opening the hot water tap until it runs steadily.

If the machine has been in an unheated store over winter, look for frost damage before pressurising anything — factory test water left in a fitting through a hard frost cracks brass, and it reveals itself as a spray at 9 bar.

## Contents against the packing list

Do this on day one, not on the day someone needs the part.

Portafilters, baskets in each size, blind basket, tamper, inlet hose, drain hose, unions, feet, the manual, and any programming or service documentation. Missing accessories are trivially replaced on a day-one call and awkward six weeks later.

Keep the crate until commissioning is complete and accepted. If the machine has to go back, it has to go back packaged.

## The pre-power-on checklist

1. Crate photographed, damage noted on the delivery note before signing.
2. Data plate voltage and phase confirmed against measured site supply.
3. All internal transit packing, ties and shipping brackets removed.
4. Electrical terminals checked for tightness; earth continuity verified and recorded.
5. Plumbing fittings checked; boiler and copper inspected for damage.
6. Relief valve free; steam and water valves smooth.
7. Group gaskets, screens and screws present and correct.
8. Boiler filled and **confirmed** filled before heating is energised.
9. Accessories checked against the packing list.
10. Serials photographed, warranty registered, crate retained.

None of this is difficult and none of it is optional on a machine worth what these machines are worth. The alternative is finding out about a loose element terminal in month eight, by which point the burnt terminal block has taken the loom with it.
