Backflushing is the highest-leverage five minutes in a coffee bar's day, and it is done incorrectly on most bars I visit. Not skipped — done, but with too much detergent and not enough rinsing, which manages to be worse than either extreme.

## What it actually does

On a machine with a three-way solenoid valve, the group has a path back out to the drain. Fit a blind basket — no holes — and the pump has nowhere to push water except backwards through the group's internals and out through that valve.

That reverse flow is scouring two things: the underside of the dispersion screen and block, and the three-way valve itself.

The valve is the real target. It spends its life switching hot, oil-laden water, and coffee oils polymerise into a varnish on its internals. A fouled three-way valve is the cause of a specific, very common set of symptoms: the group dribbles after the shot ends, pressure does not release properly, the puck is soupy when you knock it out, and eventually the valve sticks.

> You are not cleaning the machine for hygiene's sake. You are protecting the three-way valve, which is a considerably more expensive component than a bottle of detergent.

## Check the machine can be backflushed

Before establishing any routine, confirm it applies. Machines without a three-way valve — many lever machines, and some other designs — must not be backflushed. Forcing pressure against a closed system with nowhere to vent damages things.

Read the manual for the specific machine. If in doubt, ask the manufacturer. This is not a universal procedure.

## The routine

**Water only, every group, at close.** Blind basket in, run the group in short bursts — roughly five seconds on, ten seconds off — five or six times. The pause matters: it lets the valve cycle and the water reverse, which is where the scouring happens. Holding it on continuously does much less.

**With detergent, on a schedule.** Daily at close on a busy bar; two or three times a week on a quieter one. Follow the machine manufacturer's guidance where they give it.

The detergent step:

1. Blind basket in, with a **measured** dose of espresso machine cleaner.
2. Run the same short-burst cycle, five or six times.
3. Remove the portafilter, rinse it and the blind basket thoroughly under the group.
4. Refit the blind basket, empty, and run the burst cycle **at least ten more times** with clean water.
5. Remove, refit the normal basket, and flush the group.
6. Pull a shot and throw it away.

## Dose, which is where it goes wrong

Use the amount specified on the container. For most powdered espresso machine cleaners that is a small measure — commonly around half a gram to a gram, roughly a quarter teaspoon per group. Not a heaped spoonful.

More detergent does not clean better. These products are strongly alkaline, and excess does three things: it attacks group gaskets and seals, it can leave a deposit that itself restricts flow, and it takes far more rinsing to remove. Bars that use a generous scoop "to be safe" are the bars where I replace gaskets early.

Use a product intended for espresso machines. Not dishwasher detergent, not bleach, not a domestic degreaser.

## Rinsing is the step that gets cut

This is the failure I see most, and it is the one with the worst consequences.

Residual alkaline detergent in the group tastes appalling — soapy, bitter, chemically sharp — and it will be in every drink until it is flushed out. It is also caustic, so it sits against seals overnight.

Baristas at close are tired and the machine looks clean, so the rinse cycles get shortened from ten to three. The next morning's coffee tastes wrong, nobody connects it to last night's clean, and the grinder gets blamed.

If a bar reports a mystery off-taste that appears in the morning and fades through the day, ask to watch the close-down routine. It is usually the rinse.

## Also clean the parts you took off

Backflushing does not clean the basket, the portafilter or the top of the screen.

- **Soak baskets and the blind basket** in a detergent solution — same product, hot water — then brush and rinse. Do this daily.
- **Portafilters: soak the metal, keep the handle out.** Plastic and wooden handles degrade in hot alkaline solution, and the handle's fixing can loosen. Suspend them so only the metal is submerged.
- **Brush the group** with a group brush at close, before backflushing, to get the grounds off the screen and out of the gasket rim.
- **Wipe the gasket rim.** Compacted grounds in the gasket seat are what cause a group to weep.

## Symptoms of a bar that is not backflushing enough

- Bitter, rancid, ashy notes in every drink, unresponsive to recipe changes.
- The group dribbling for a long time after the shot ends.
- A soupy or sloppy puck at knock-out.
- Slow group flow at a grind setting that used to be fine.
- Visible dark residue when the dispersion screen finally comes off.

## And a bar doing it too aggressively

- Group gaskets failing early, or going hard and shiny.
- A soapy or metallic taste that comes and goes.
- Seals swelling or extruding.

Both failure modes come from the same place: nobody was given a specific quantity and a specific number of rinse cycles.

## Write it down with numbers in it

The routine survives staff turnover only if it exists as instructions with quantities. "Backflush the machine" decays within a month. What works, laminated by the machine:

> **At close, each group:** 6 × (5 s on, 10 s off) water only. Then ¼ tsp cleaner, 6 × (5 s on, 10 s off). Remove and rinse portafilter. Then 10 × (5 s on, 10 s off) clean water. Refit normal basket, flush, discard one shot.

Numbers survive. Adjectives do not.
