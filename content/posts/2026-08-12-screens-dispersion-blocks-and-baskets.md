Backflushing handles the group's internals. It does very little for the parts that actually touch the coffee, and those parts are consumables that most bars treat as permanent fixtures.

The pattern is always the same: extraction drifts slowly over months, the team compensates with grind and dose, and nobody suspects the hardware because it looks fine.

## The dispersion screen

The perforated disc at the bottom of the group. Its job is to spread water evenly across the whole puck surface.

Coffee oils and fines block the perforations progressively, and they do not block evenly. A screen with a clear centre and clogged edges delivers water preferentially to the middle of the puck — which is channelling, produced by the machine rather than by the barista's distribution.

**Remove it weekly** on a moderate bar, more often at volume. Soak in espresso machine detergent solution, brush both faces, rinse thoroughly, and hold it up to the light. Every hole should show light. If a soak does not clear it, replace it — screens are inexpensive.

Two things to check while it is off:

- **Flatness.** Screens dish over time, from repeated tamping pressure transmitted through the puck and from over-tightened screws. A dished screen sits proud in the middle and holds water at the edges. Lay it on something flat. If it rocks, replace it.
- **The retaining screw.** These seize, and they are usually brass or stainless in an aluminium or brass block, hot and wet for years. Use the correct driver and seat it fully — a burred screw head turns a five-minute job into drilling out a fastener in a group head. Replace the screw whenever it looks tired. They cost almost nothing.

## The dispersion block

Behind the screen. This is where the serious deposit lives, and it is the part almost nobody removes.

The block has internal passages that distribute water from the group inlet across the screen. Oils accumulate in those passages, and on hard water there is scale as well. Backflushing scours the face of the block but does very little inside it.

**Remove and inspect it quarterly**, or twice a year on a light bar. Expect to find a hard dark layer on the first inspection of a machine that has never had it off. Soak and brush the passages, check for scale, and look for pitting or erosion around the water inlet — erosion there changes the distribution pattern permanently and means a replacement.

This is also the best available scale inspection on a machine you cannot see inside. Scale on the dispersion block means scale in the boiler, and that is a water conversation rather than a cleaning one.

## Baskets are consumables

The hardest idea to sell to an owner, and the one with the clearest effect on the cup.

A basket is a precision component. Its hole count, hole diameter and hole distribution determine flow resistance. In service, three things happen:

- **Holes enlarge and lose their edge** from abrasion by grounds and repeated cleaning.
- **The rim deforms** from knocking out against a bar, which is the fastest way to ruin one.
- **The wall distorts** slightly from tamping and handling.

The result is a basket that flows faster at the same grind, and flows *unevenly* because the wear is not uniform. The bar compensates by grinding finer. Extraction is now happening differently across the puck, and no recipe change fixes it.

**Retire baskets on a schedule.** On a busy bar, annually. On a very busy bar, twice a year. This is cheap relative to the cost of six months of slightly wrong coffee.

Practical ways to catch a worn basket without gauges:

- **Compare against a new one.** Keep one new basket sealed in a drawer as a reference. Same coffee, same dose, same grind, same machine — if flow time differs meaningfully, the working basket has moved.
- **Watch for a bar creeping finer** over months with no other explanation. That is the signature.
- **Look at the rim** for dents and burrs, and at the holes under a bright light for enlargement or blockage.

Any basket that has been knocked out against something hard, or dropped, goes in the bin. The rim seals against the group gasket; a deformed rim is a leak and an uneven puck.

## Portafilters

- **Check the retaining spring or clip.** A tired spring lets the basket sit loose or lift slightly under pressure, which produces intermittent, unrepeatable bad shots — the hardest kind to diagnose.
- **Clear the spouts.** They block with old coffee and split the flow oddly. Brush them through.
- **Check for a bent or worn lug** on the portafilter body. Worn lugs change how the basket seats against the gasket.
- **Keep handles out of soak solution**, as covered in the backflushing routine.

## A workable schedule

| Interval | Task |
| --- | --- |
| Daily | Brush group and gasket rim; soak baskets and blind basket; rinse and inspect portafilters |
| Weekly | Remove dispersion screens, soak, brush, check against the light, check flatness |
| Monthly | Inspect basket rims and holes against the reference basket; check portafilter springs |
| Quarterly | Remove dispersion blocks, clean internal passages, inspect for scale and erosion |
| Annually | Replace baskets; replace screens and retaining screws as needed |

## The diagnostic worth remembering

When a bar says extraction has gone strange and nothing has changed, the order I check is: basket, screen, gasket seat, dispersion block. All four are cheap, all four are quick, and all four change flow distribution.

It is a much better first move than adjusting the grinder — because if the hardware has drifted, dialling the grinder around it just hides the problem under a second variable.
