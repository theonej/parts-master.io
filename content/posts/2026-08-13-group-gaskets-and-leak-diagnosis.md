The group gasket is a small rubber ring that costs very little and, when it goes, produces symptoms that get blamed on everything else. It is also the wearing part with the clearest early warning signal, and almost nobody watches for it.

## The handle-position tell

This is the diagnostic worth teaching every barista.

When a gasket is new, the portafilter locks home at a particular handle angle — often somewhere around the 5 o'clock or 6 o'clock position, depending on the machine. Note where that is on a fresh gasket.

As the gasket compresses and hardens with age, the portafilter has to rotate further to seal. The handle creeps around. When it has travelled to roughly perpendicular, or past the point where the barista is having to lean on it, the gasket is finished.

> Mark the new-gasket handle position on the drip tray with a piece of tape on installation day. When the handle reaches the second mark, order gaskets. It is the cheapest condition monitoring available.

Expect six to twelve months on a busy bar. Heat cycling hardens the rubber, and alkaline detergent accelerates it — which is one more reason to get the backflush dose right.

## Symptoms of a failed gasket

- Water squirting or dribbling from the side of the portafilter *during* extraction.
- The handle travelling well past its original lock position.
- Real effort needed to lock in, or a squeak as it seats.
- A hiss from the group under pressure.
- Grounds spraying out at the rim.

## Changing one properly

The part that matters is not removing the gasket — it is not damaging the seat.

1. **Machine off, group depressurised, and cool enough to work on.** The gasket sits in a hot brass groove; do this on a cold machine.
2. **Remove the dispersion screen and screw first.** On most groups the screen retains part of the assembly, and levering the gasket with the screen in place risks bending it.
3. **Extract the old gasket.** A proper gasket hook or removal tool is worth owning. If you use a screwdriver, take great care: **a scored or gouged group seat will never seal again**, and repairing it means machining or replacing the group. Lever against the gasket, never against the brass.
4. **Clean the seat completely.** Compacted grounds, old rubber and scale in the groove are the most common reason a *new* gasket leaks. Scrape it out, brush it, wipe it.
5. **Fit the correct gasket.** They come in specific thicknesses — 8 mm, 8.5 mm, 9 mm are all common — and the right one for the machine is the one specified for it. A thicker gasket does not "seal better"; it changes where the handle locks and how the basket sits against the screen.
6. **Watch the orientation.** Many gaskets are chamfered or tapered rather than symmetrical. The taper goes a particular way. Fitting one upside down produces a gasket that seals poorly and fails early.
7. **Seat it evenly**, all the way round, then refit the screen and screw.
8. **Test.** Lock in a blind basket and run the pump. Note the new handle position and mark it.

If you lubricate at all, use only a food-grade silicone intended for the purpose, sparingly. Petroleum grease destroys the rubber.

## Not every group leak is the gasket

This is where diagnosis matters, because replacing a good gasket does not fix anything and the second replacement gets blamed on the part.

**Leaks only while extracting, at the portafilter rim.** Gasket, basket rim, or portafilter lugs. Check the basket for a deformed rim and the portafilter lugs for wear before assuming the gasket. A dented basket rim leaks past a perfect gasket.

**Dribbles from the group for a long time after the shot ends.** Three-way valve, fouled or worn. This is not a gasket. Backflush properly and, if it persists, the valve needs service.

**Water running from the group continuously, machine idle.** A solenoid not closing, or debris on its seat. Upstream of the gasket entirely.

**Water appearing under the machine, unrelated to shots.** Boiler, a fitting, the heat exchanger, or the pump. Find it before running the machine — dry everything, put paper down, and watch where it wets first.

**Steam rather than water, from the group area.** Usually the group-to-boiler connection, or an over-pressure condition. Treat as urgent.

## The trap: tightening harder

When a group starts weeping, the instinctive fix is to lock the portafilter in harder. It works, briefly.

It also accelerates everything: it crushes the tired gasket faster, wears the portafilter lugs, wears the group's locking ring, and puts strain into the group casting. Bars that have been leaning on the handle for months arrive with worn lugs as well as a dead gasket, and lugs are not a cheap part.

Teach the team that increasing effort to lock in is a *symptom to report*, not a technique to adopt.

## Keep them in stock

Group gaskets are the definitive example of a part to hold on the shelf. They cost very little, they fail on a predictable schedule, and being without one means a group out of service on a Saturday.

Stock a set per group, plus dispersion screens and screws in the same drawer, since you will be in there anyway. Change them all as a set on an annual service and the group behaves like new.
