Everything else on the machine handles water and coffee. The steam wand handles milk, which is a food-safety problem as well as an engineering one, and it is the component most likely to be found dirty on an inspection.

It is also the circuit where neglect produces the fastest measurable decline in drink quality, because a partially blocked tip cannot texture milk properly no matter how good the barista is.

## Suck-back is the mechanism to understand

When the steam valve closes, the steam remaining in the wand condenses. Condensing steam collapses in volume, which creates a partial vacuum, which draws whatever the tip is sitting in back up inside the wand.

If the tip is still in the jug when the valve closes, that is milk — drawn up into the wand, where it sits at body temperature in the dark.

This single mechanism explains most steam wand problems: blocked tip holes, the smell, the hygiene failures, and the crust that forms inside the wand tube.

The fix is behavioural and takes two seconds: **take the tip out of the milk before closing the valve, then purge immediately.**

## The routine, every single time

1. **Purge before use.** Clears condensate so you are not diluting the milk with water.
2. **Texture the milk.**
3. **Lift the wand clear of the jug, then close the valve.** In that order.
4. **Wipe immediately**, with a dedicated cloth, while the residue is still liquid. Milk that dries on is a different job.
5. **Purge again**, hard, for a couple of seconds.

Steps 3 and 5 are the ones that get dropped under pressure, and they are the ones that matter.

**A dedicated wand cloth.** Not the counter cloth. Kept in sanitiser, changed regularly, used for nothing else. Wiping a steam wand with the cloth that just wiped the counter is a straightforward cross-contamination route, and it is an easy finding for an inspector.

Never use a scourer or abrasive pad on a wand. It scratches the stainless, and those scratches hold residue and are far harder to clean than the polished surface was.

## Tips

The tip's hole pattern determines how the steam enters the milk. Partially blocked holes give weak, asymmetric, unpredictable texturing — the barista fights it and blames technique.

**Daily:** purge and wipe as above.

**Weekly or more:** remove the tip and soak it in a milk-line cleaner — an alkaline product designed for protein and fat, not a general degreaser. Soak hot, then check every hole against the light.

If a hole is still blocked, clear it with something *smaller* than the hole — a fine wire or a proper tip-cleaning pin. Never drill it, never ream it with something oversized. Enlarging a hole permanently changes the steam pattern and the tip's balance, and tips are cheap enough that replacement is always the better answer.

Check the tip's sealing face and thread when it is off. A tip that has been cross-threaded or over-tightened will leak steam around the joint.

## The whole assembly, periodically

The tip is not the only place milk gets. Suck-back puts it up inside the wand tube.

Every week or two, remove the wand from the machine where the design allows and soak the whole tube in milk-line cleaner. Push a flexible brush through it. On machines where the wand does not come off easily, at minimum submerge the tip end in cleaner solution and draw the solution up the tube by opening and closing the valve — the suck-back that causes the problem can be used to fix it.

What comes out of a wand that has never been done this way is persuasive. Do it once in front of an owner who thinks it is unnecessary.

## The valve

Steam valves are mechanical, they work hot, and their seals wear out. Symptoms and what they mean:

- **Dripping or seeping when fully closed.** The valve seat or its seal is worn. It will not improve, and the usual response — closing it harder — crushes the seal faster and can damage the seat.
- **Stiff or gritty to operate.** The stem is scaling or the packing has dried out. Needs stripping, cleaning and re-greasing with a high-temperature food-safe lubricant.
- **Steam escaping around the stem** rather than from the tip. Stem packing or O-ring.
- **Will not shut off at all.** Isolate the machine. This is not a keep-going fault.

Valve rebuild kits are inexpensive and worth holding in stock — seals, O-rings and a seat. Rebuilding a valve on a scheduled service is a twenty-minute job; doing it after the seat is damaged means replacing the valve body.

Scale is the accelerant here as everywhere: a machine on properly treated water has valves that last, and a machine on hard water has valves that seize.

## Wet steam is a symptom, not a characteristic

If the wand spits water along with steam, or the milk gets thin and watery while texturing, something is wrong upstream.

- **Boiler over-filled.** The most common cause. The level probe or autofill is letting the water level rise above where the steam is drawn off, so you are pulling water with the steam. Check level and probe.
- **Steam pressure too low.** Check the gauge against its set point.
- **Condensate not purged** before use — the easy one, and it is a routine problem rather than a fault.

Persistent wet steam with a correct water level and pressure is worth investigating properly; it usually means a level sensing problem that is about to become a bigger one.

## Insulated wands still need all of this

Cool-touch and insulated wands are excellent for burns and change nothing about the cleaning requirement. The sleeve can also hide dried milk at the joint between sleeve and tube, so check under it periodically — it is a spot that gets missed precisely because it is covered.
