Burrs wear out. Everyone accepts this in principle and almost nobody tracks it, because the wear is gradual and the bar compensates without noticing.

The classic history: a bar opens, dials in at setting 4.2, and eighteen months later is running 3.1 on the same coffee and cannot work out why the shots are muddy. Nothing broke. The burrs just got blunt one dose at a time.

## Measure life in kilograms

Months are meaningless. A grinder doing 2 kg a day and one doing 15 kg a day are on completely different schedules.

Indicative figures, which vary substantially by manufacturer, material and roast:

| Burr set | Rough life |
| --- | --- |
| 58–64 mm flat, steel | 250–400 kg |
| 71–75 mm flat, steel | 400–700 kg |
| 83–98 mm flat, steel | 800–1200 kg |
| 64–68 mm conical, steel | 400–600 kg |
| 71–85 mm conical, steel | 700–1000 kg |
| Titanium or DLC coated | Roughly double the steel figure |

Treat these as order-of-magnitude planning numbers and ask your manufacturer for theirs. Dark oily roasts and any grit in the beans shorten them.

The practical consequence: **log throughput.** Bean orders already tell you kilograms purchased, so it costs nothing to keep a running total per grinder. Without that number, burr replacement is guesswork, and guesswork always errs toward "later".

> One line in the maintenance log — date, kilograms through since last burr change — turns burr replacement from an argument into a scheduled task.

## The signature of a worn set

Worn burrs do not stop working. They fracture coffee differently: more fines, a broader particle distribution, less clean cutting and more crushing.

What that looks like on the bar:

- **The grind setting creeping finer over months** to hold the same shot time. This is the single clearest indicator.
- **More fines,** which means more channelling and less repeatable shots.
- **Increased clumping** at the same setting.
- **Longer grind time per dose,** because blunt burrs cut more slowly.
- **The grinder running hotter,** because more energy goes into crushing rather than cutting.
- **A flat, muddy, low-clarity cup** — bitterness without definition, and a shorter finish.

The trap is that a worn *basket* produces a very similar "creeping finer" signature. So when a bar reports it, check both: compare the basket against a sealed reference basket, and check the burr throughput figure. They are both cheap consumables and both worth eliminating early.

## Inspecting burrs

Take them out, brush them clean and dry, and look at the **outer cutting teeth** — the fine ones at the perimeter that do the final sizing. That is where wear shows first and matters most.

- **Under a bright light or a loupe**, compare the cutting edges to a new burr if you have one. New edges are crisp, defined, and reflect light as a thin bright line. Worn edges are rounded and reflect as a broad shiny land.
- **Shiny wear lands** on the flanks are wear, not polish.
- **The fingernail test.** Run a fingernail lightly across a cutting edge. A sharp burr catches at the nail. A worn one slides. It is crude, it is subjective, and it is genuinely useful once you have felt both.
- **Look for chips.** A single chipped tooth from a stone or a staple ruins consistency immediately, regardless of how much life is left. Any impact damage means replacement.

Keep one new burr set in a drawer as a reference for exactly this comparison. It doubles as the spare, so it is not an extra cost.

## Replacing them

- **Always as a matched set.** Both burrs, together. Mixing a new burr with a worn one gives you the worse behaviour of the two and uneven wear from the start.
- **Never reinstall old burrs** "as a spare". They are scrap. A worn set in a drawer will eventually get fitted by someone in a hurry.
- **Mark or note the setting before stripping**, as with cleaning. You will still re-dial, but you want a starting point.
- **Clean the chamber, carrier and chute while it is open.** It is the same job.
- **Check the carrier and threads** for wear and for coffee packed into the mounting faces. A burr bolted onto a bed of compacted grounds is not sitting flat, which brings us to alignment — tomorrow's post.
- **Torque the fixings properly** and evenly.

## Expect a break-in period

New burrs are sharp and slightly rough, and they change over the first few kilograms as the edges settle. Grind will shift during that window.

Plan for it: replace burrs at the end of a week rather than before a busy morning, dial in fresh afterwards, and warn the team that the setting will move for a few days. A bar that changes burrs on a Saturday morning has a bad Saturday.

## The cost argument

Burrs feel expensive as a line item and are trivial as a proportion of what runs through them.

A 71 mm set might be replaced after 500 kg of coffee. Against the wholesale value of 500 kg of specialty coffee, the burrs are a rounding error — and the last 100 kg through a worn set was measurably worse coffee, sold at full price to customers who noticed something was off.

That is the framing that gets budget approved: worn burrs are not saving money, they are degrading inventory that has already been paid for.

## What to hold

One spare matched set per grinder, on the shelf. It serves as the wear reference, it covers the chipped-burr emergency, and it removes the lead-time excuse for running a worn set for another month.
