A flat burr set works by holding two discs a precise distance apart. The whole premise is that the gap is the same everywhere around the circumference.

When it is not — when one side sits fractionally closer than the other — the grinder is producing two grind settings simultaneously and blending them. The result is a wider particle distribution than the burrs are capable of, which shows up as more fines, more channelling and less clarity in the cup.

## Check for dirt first

Before reaching for shims, understand that **most apparent misalignment is compacted coffee under a burr.**

Coffee packs into the mounting faces and behind the burr seat. A few tenths of a millimetre of compressed grounds under one edge tilts the burr just as effectively as a manufacturing tolerance, and it is free to fix.

So the sequence is always: strip, clean the burr faces and the carrier mounting surfaces back to bare metal, check the mounting faces for burrs or damage, reassemble with the fixings torqued evenly, *then* test alignment. A surprising number of grinders come into spec at that point.

## The marker test

The standard field method, and it works.

1. Remove the burrs and clean everything properly.
2. Coat the cutting surface of one burr with permanent marker — an even layer across the full face, inner teeth to outer.
3. Refit, torque the fixings evenly.
4. With the grinder **empty**, close the setting until the burrs just begin to contact, briefly. You want the lightest possible touch for the shortest possible time.
5. Back off, remove the burr, and read the marker.

**Reading it:**

- Marker removed evenly all the way round the outer edge — aligned.
- Marker removed on one side only, or across an arc — misaligned, and the cleared side is the close side.
- Marker removed only at the inner teeth — usually a seating or carrier problem rather than a tilt.

> Bringing burrs into contact under power risks damaging the cutting edges, which defeats the purpose. Do it as briefly as possible, at the lowest speed the grinder allows, and where the design permits, rotate by hand instead of running the motor.

If you would rather not touch the burrs together at all, a dial indicator on the burr carrier — rotated through a full turn, reading runout — gives you the same information without contact. It needs a way to mount the indicator, which is not always available in the field.

## Shimming

Correction means packing the **low** side out so the burr sits parallel: thin shims under the burr or under its carrier, on the side where the marker was *not* removed.

- Use proper shim material — thin steel or mylar shim stock, or the manufacturer's shims where they exist. Not paper, not tape.
- Start thin. Shim stock in the 0.05–0.1 mm range is the usual working range; the correction needed is normally very small.
- Shim, reassemble, torque evenly, re-test with fresh marker. It is iterative, and two or three cycles is normal.
- Watch that the shim does not intrude into the grinding path or foul the fixing.
- Stop when the marker clears evenly. Do not chase perfection past the point the test can resolve.

Some grinders have adjustable burr carriers designed for this, with three adjustment points. If yours does, use the mechanism rather than shims.

**Write the shim stack down.** Position, thickness, count — in the maintenance log. When burrs are replaced, the existing shims are a starting point, and without a record the next person starts from nothing.

## Torque and sequence matter

Burr fixings should be tightened evenly, in a cross pattern, in stages, like a wheel. Tightening one screw fully before starting the next pulls the burr out of plane, and you will chase an alignment error that you created during assembly.

Re-check the marker after final torque, not before.

## What to expect from it

Honest expectations, because this topic attracts overclaiming.

A genuinely misaligned grinder that gets corrected improves noticeably: tighter distribution, fewer fines, often the ability to run slightly coarser for the same shot time, and better clarity. That is a real gain and worth the hour.

A grinder that was already within tolerance gains nothing, because there was nothing to correct. Manufacturing quality varies, and some grinders arrive aligned.

Alignment is also not a substitute for the basics. Sharp burrs, a clean chamber, consistent dose and decent water all outrank it. If a bar has worn burrs and a dirty chute, shimming the burrs is the wrong job to do first.

## Conicals

Conical geometry is largely self-centring — the inner cone sits inside the outer ring and the geometry constrains the relationship. There is far less to align, and field shimming is not usually a meaningful intervention.

If a conical grinder is producing an uneven grind, look at burr wear, chipping, chamber cleanliness, carrier seating and bearing play rather than alignment.

## When to do it

- On commissioning a new flat-burr grinder, as part of the baseline — so you know where it started.
- Whenever burrs are replaced, since new burrs sit on the same carrier and the shim requirement may change.
- When a grinder produces a wider distribution than it should, after cleanliness and wear have been eliminated.
- Not routinely. It is a diagnostic and corrective task, not a recurring one.

## The order of operations

If a bar complains their flat-burr grinder is inconsistent, this is the sequence, cheapest and most likely first:

1. Dose consistency — weigh ten doses.
2. Chamber, chute and burr faces cleaned properly.
3. Burr wear checked and throughput reviewed.
4. Fixings torqued evenly, mounting faces clean and undamaged.
5. Alignment tested with a marker.
6. Shimmed if, and only if, the test shows a real error.

Working in that order means you find the cheap problem first, which is nearly always the one that was actually causing the complaint.
