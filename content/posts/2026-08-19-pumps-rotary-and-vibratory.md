Pump faults get misdiagnosed more than almost anything else, because the symptom — low or unstable brew pressure — has half a dozen causes and only one of them is the pump.

The expensive mistake is fitting a new pump to a machine whose inlet filter is blocked. The new pump reads correctly for a fortnight and then behaves exactly like the old one.

## The two types

**Rotary vane.** A vane pump driven by an external motor. Quiet, smooth, rated for continuous duty, and the standard on commercial machines. Brew pressure is set by an adjustable bypass. It needs adequate inlet pressure — typically at least 1–2 bar — and it is lubricated by the water passing through it, so running it dry damages it immediately.

**Vibratory.** An oscillating armature and piston. Cheap, compact, noisy, and duty-limited — they are designed for intermittent use, and they get hot when worked hard. Common on single-group and domestic machines. Output pressure varies with flow rate, so pressure is inherently less stable than a rotary. Usually not field-adjustable.

## Setting brew pressure

The procedure that matters, and the one most often done wrong.

**Set it under flow.** A gauge reading taken with the group blocked and no water moving is a static reading, and it is not the pressure the puck experiences. Use a portafilter pressure gauge, with water flowing, at the group.

1. Fit the portafilter gauge to the group.
2. Run the group and let it settle for a few seconds.
3. Read the pressure while flowing. Target around 9 bar unless the manufacturer specifies otherwise.
4. Adjust the rotary pump's bypass screw in small increments. Clockwise raises on most pumps — confirm for yours.
5. Re-run and re-read after each adjustment.
6. Check the other groups. They should read closely; a large discrepancy between groups is a group-side restriction, not a pump setting.

> If you find yourself opening the bypass a long way to reach 9 bar, stop. You are compensating for a worn pump or a starved inlet, and you are about to mask the real fault.

## Diagnosing low pressure

Work from the inlet forward. It is the cheap end.

1. **Line pressure at the machine inlet,** measured. If the supply is low, nothing downstream can be right.
2. **The water filter cartridge.** A cartridge at the end of its life is the single most common cause of low brew pressure and starved pumps. Check its date and volume against the log.
3. **Inlet screens and the pump's own strainer.** Often a small mesh that silts up.
4. **Any partially closed isolation valve.** Someone shut it 80% during a repair and never reopened it.
5. **Softener or treatment stage restriction.**
6. **Bypass setting.** Now, not first.
7. **Pump wear.** Vanes wear and internal clearances open up, so the pump can no longer make pressure at flow. The signature is a pump that holds pressure statically but drops away as soon as water moves.
8. **A leak on the pressure side,** including a passing expansion valve or a weeping fitting.

## Cavitation, and why the inlet matters so much

If the pump cannot get water in as fast as it is trying to push it out, the pressure at the inlet drops low enough for vapour bubbles to form and then collapse violently inside the pump.

That is cavitation, and it destroys pumps — mechanically, from the inside, quickly.

The sound is distinctive: a harsh rattling or gravelly noise, quite different from the pump's normal hum, often with the pressure needle flickering. Treat it as an immediate stop-and-diagnose condition rather than a noise to live with.

Almost every cavitating pump I have seen was starved by a blocked filter cartridge or low line pressure. Which means: **a neglected filter change eventually costs a pump**, and that is a useful thing to tell a client who thinks cartridges are optional.

## Other pump symptoms

**Pressure spikes above setpoint.** Often thermal expansion with nowhere to go — a check valve or backflow preventer on the inlet making a closed system. The fix is an expansion vessel, not a pump adjustment. This is the plumbing detail from the water post, showing up as a pump complaint.

**Loud humming or vibration through the whole machine.** Frequently the pump mount rather than the pump: a shipping bracket left fitted, a failed rubber mount, or the pump touching the frame or a panel. Check before condemning the pump.

**Motor runs, no pressure at all.** A failed coupling between motor and pump on a rotary. Look at the coupling before anything else; the flexible element wears out and it is a cheap part.

**Pump gets hot and cuts out.** On a vibratory pump, likely duty-cycle related — it is being asked to do a commercial job. On a rotary, check the motor for a failing bearing or an overload condition.

**Pressure fine at one group, low at another.** Not the pump. A group-side restriction: gicleur, dispersion block, or solenoid.

## Maintenance

There is not much to do, which is why it gets ignored entirely.

- **Change filter cartridges on schedule.** This is the main pump maintenance task, and it happens somewhere else in the machine.
- **Keep the inlet strainer clean.** Include it in the annual service.
- **Never run dry.** After any work that empties the inlet side, confirm water is present before energising.
- **Check the coupling** on rotary pumps annually, and the mounts for perishing.
- **Record brew pressure under flow** at every service. A slow decline over years is pump wear becoming visible, and it lets you plan a replacement rather than react to a failure.

## Rebuild or replace

Rotary pumps can sometimes be rebuilt, but on commercial machines the economics usually favour replacement — the part is not expensive relative to the labour, and a rebuilt pump of unknown internal condition is a poor thing to leave behind on a busy bar.

Keep the old one as an emergency spare only if it still made pressure when it came off. A cavitation-damaged pump is scrap, and it should go in the bin rather than the spares drawer where someone will fit it in a hurry.
