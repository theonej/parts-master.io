Inside each group's water path there is a small restrictor — the gicleur, or jet — with an orifice typically somewhere between 0.5 and 0.8 mm. It limits how fast water can enter the group.

It is one of the smallest parts on the machine and one of the most consequential, because it determines how quickly the puck gets saturated and therefore how the early phase of every extraction behaves. It is also a half-millimetre hole in a hot water circuit, which means it blocks.

## Why one group tastes different

This is the fault that hides in plain sight on multi-group bars.

Each group has its own gicleur, its own dispersion block and its own solenoid. They wear and foul independently. So group one can end up flowing meaningfully slower than group three, and the shots differ — same grinder, same dose, same recipe, different coffee in the cup.

It stays hidden because of how bars work. Baristas settle into positions. The person on the left works group one all shift and dials the grinder to suit it; the person on the right works group three. Each thinks the machine is fine. The difference only surfaces as an argument about whose shots taste better, or as inconsistent customer complaints nobody can reproduce.

> Comparing groups against each other is the single most productive diagnostic on a multi-group machine, and it costs thirty seconds per group.

## The test

Simple, repeatable, needs a jug and a timer.

1. Machine at temperature, no portafilter — or an empty portafilter with no basket, depending on the machine.
2. Run the group for a fixed 30 seconds into a jug.
3. Weigh or measure what you collected.
4. Repeat for every group under identical conditions.
5. Compare, and compare against the manufacturer's specification if they publish one.

What you are looking for is **agreement between groups**. The absolute figure matters less than the spread — if two groups match and one is 20% down, you have found something real.

Record the figures in the baseline at commissioning. Then any future test is a comparison rather than a guess about what normal was.

## What makes it drift

**Blockage — slower flow.** The common direction.

- Scale, on any machine whose water treatment is not right. A 0.6 mm orifice does not need much deposit to lose a meaningful fraction of its area.
- Coffee fines that have migrated back up during backflushing.
- Debris from elsewhere in the machine — a fragment of an old gasket, corrosion product, or grit that came in with a repair.

**Erosion — faster flow.** Less common, and easier to miss because nothing looks wrong.

Years of high-velocity water through a small orifice enlarges it. The group then floods the puck faster than it should, pre-infusion effectively disappears, and channelling increases. The bar responds by grinding finer, which is the same signature as worn burrs and worn baskets — which is why this test belongs in the diagnostic sequence.

## Cleaning and replacing

- Isolate water, depressurise, and let the group cool.
- Remove the gicleur — location varies; on many machines it is behind the group inlet fitting or within the dispersion assembly.
- Clean it with solvent, a soak, and a soft bristle. **Do not run anything through the orifice that could enlarge it,** and never drill or ream it. The dimension is the specification.
- Inspect the orifice under a light. A crisp round hole is good. A bellmouthed, oval or visibly enlarged hole means replacement.
- Refit with the correct seal, and re-test flow.

Gicleurs are inexpensive. If in any doubt about the orifice condition, fit a new one — it is a fraction of the cost of the visit.

Keep a set on the shelf, and note the size fitted. Some machines use different sizes for different applications, and a well-meaning previous engineer may have changed one.

## Differential diagnosis

Slow flow at one group has several possible causes and the gicleur is only one. Check in this order, cheapest and most reversible first:

1. **Dispersion screen** — blocked, dished, or clogged. Free to check.
2. **Dispersion block** — internal passages fouled or scaled.
3. **Gicleur** — the orifice itself.
4. **Solenoid valve** — a partially blocked inlet solenoid, or one not opening fully.
5. **Thermosyphon or group inlet line restriction**, on machines with them.

If *all* groups are slow together, it is not group-side at all — look upstream at the filter cartridge, line pressure and pump, in that order. That is the previous post's territory, and the distinction between "one group" and "all groups" tells you which post to be reading.

## Why it matters beyond the numbers

Flow rate sets how the puck is wetted. Slower flow means a gentler ramp, more effective pre-infusion, and more time for the bed to settle before full pressure. Faster flow slams the bed and provokes channelling.

That is why two machines with identical pump pressure and identical brew temperature still produce different shots at the same grind, and why moving a recipe between machines rarely transfers cleanly. The gicleur is a large part of a machine's character, and it is not on any spec sheet a customer sees.

## Put it in the service routine

Group flow comparison belongs in every scheduled service, and it takes two minutes for a three-group machine.

It catches scale early — before the boiler is compromised — because a small orifice in the brew path fouls long before anything else shows symptoms. In that respect it is a free early-warning system for the water treatment, which is where this month's writing started.
