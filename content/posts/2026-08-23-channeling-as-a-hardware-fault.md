Channelling — water finding a low-resistance path through the puck instead of extracting it evenly — is treated as a preparation problem. Distribution, tamping, dose. And often it is.

But a machine can produce channelling entirely on its own, and when it does, no amount of training fixes it. I have walked into bars where a demoralised team had spent two months being coached on distribution technique while a dished dispersion screen sent water down one side of every basket.

## The test that separates them

Use a bottomless portafilter and watch where the spurt appears.

**The key observation is not whether it channels, but whether it channels in the same place.**

- **Same location, shot after shot, across different baristas** — hardware. A person's technique is not that repeatable. Something in the machine is directing water the same wrong way every time.
- **Random locations, varying between baristas** — preparation. Different people, different results, different spots.

That distinction takes ten shots and it redirects the entire investigation.

The second test is just as quick: have **one** barista pull the same recipe with the same prep on two different groups. If one group channels and the other does not, the variable is not the human.

> Before any retraining programme, run those two tests. If channelling is positionally consistent or group-specific, it is a hardware fault and coaching will not touch it.

## Hardware causes, roughly in order of frequency

**The dispersion screen.** Partially blocked, or dished. A screen with clear centre holes and clogged edge holes concentrates flow in the middle. A dished screen holds water unevenly. Free to check, cheap to replace, and the most common cause on this list.

**A worn or deformed basket.** Holes enlarged unevenly, or a rim dented from knocking out. The flow resistance is now different in different parts of the basket, and the water goes where the resistance is lowest.

**A tired portafilter spring or retaining clip.** If the basket can lift or shift slightly under 9 bar, the seal at the rim breaks and water bypasses the puck at the edge. This one is genuinely nasty to diagnose because it is intermittent — it produces occasional inexplicable shots rather than consistent ones.

**A tamper that does not match the basket.** A tamper appreciably smaller than the basket's internal diameter leaves an unconsolidated ring of coffee around the edge, which is a channel by construction. Measure the basket and match the tamper — this is a specification error, not a technique error.

**The machine is not level.** Genuinely. If the machine sits tilted on the counter, every puck is tilted, the water bed is deeper on one side, and every shot channels the same direction. Put a spirit level on the drip tray. It takes fifteen seconds, it is checked almost never, and when it is wrong it explains everything — including the positional consistency in the test above.

**An eroded gicleur.** Flow too fast into the group, so the bed gets slammed before it can settle. From the previous post: eroded orifices are easy to miss because nothing looks broken.

**Brew pressure too high.** Above about 9 bar, on a fine grind, the pressure differential itself starts provoking channels. Check pressure under flow.

**Misaligned or worn burrs.** Excess fines migrate and block locally, then release. This shows up as inconsistency rather than a fixed location, so it can masquerade as a technique problem — which is why burr condition belongs on the list.

**Brew temperature too high.** Aggressive early gas release from very hot water disturbs the bed.

## The diagnostic order

Cheapest and most reversible first:

1. **Spirit level on the drip tray.** Fifteen seconds.
2. **Dispersion screen** out, held to the light, checked for flatness.
3. **Basket** compared against a sealed reference basket; rim inspected.
4. **Portafilter spring** and lugs checked for play.
5. **Tamper diameter** measured against basket internal diameter.
6. **Brew pressure** measured under flow at the group.
7. **Group flow rate** measured and compared across groups.
8. **Brew temperature** measured at the group, not read off the display.
9. **Burr condition, cleanliness and alignment.**

Steps one through five cost nothing and catch most of it.

## Puck screens

Worth a note, since they come up whenever channelling does.

A puck screen sits on top of the coffee bed and spreads incoming water. They genuinely help, and on a machine with an imperfect dispersion screen they can substantially even out the flow.

That is also the objection. A puck screen that fixes channelling is compensating for a hardware fault you have not found — and it will keep compensating, quietly, while the underlying fault remains and gets worse. Fine as a deliberate choice; poor as a diagnosis.

If you introduce puck screens, do it *after* the hardware checks, not instead of them.

## Say it plainly to the client

The reason this matters beyond the coffee: a team being coached on a fault they cannot fix loses confidence, and eventually stops believing feedback about their technique at all.

If the tests point at hardware, say so clearly, to the owner and to the baristas. "This is the machine, not you" is worth a lot, and it is the correct finding to report — the two-minute test is what earns the right to say it.
