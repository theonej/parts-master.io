There is a widespread belief that descaling is a routine maintenance task, like changing a filter. On a domestic machine, roughly. On a high-end commercial machine, descaling is what you do when prevention has already failed, and it carries real risk of making things worse.

The month opened with water treatment for exactly this reason: the honest position is that a properly specified machine on properly specified water should never need descaling in its service life.

## What in-situ descaling actually does

You circulate an acid through a machine to dissolve carbonate deposit. Four things happen alongside the intended one.

**Scale is loosened, not only dissolved.** Deposit comes away in flakes and fragments before it fully dissolves. Those fragments travel — into gicleurs with 0.6 mm orifices, into solenoid valve seats, into flowmeters, into the three-way valve. A machine that worked poorly before descaling can have a jammed valve and a blocked group afterwards, and the fault is now acute rather than gradual.

**Acid attacks more than scale.** Gaskets, O-rings and seals throughout the circuit are exposed to something they were not designed for. Any aluminium in the machine — and there is aluminium in more machines than people expect — is vulnerable. Brass can be dezincified by the wrong acid at the wrong concentration.

**The machine leaks afterwards.** This is the classic, and it is worth understanding properly. Scale had been sitting in a corroded pinhole or a marginal joint, acting as a plug. Remove the scale and the underlying corrosion is exposed.

The descale did not cause the leak — it revealed a defect that already existed. But that distinction is difficult to explain to an owner whose machine was working yesterday and is leaking today, which is precisely why you have the conversation **before** the work rather than after.

**Probes and sensors get disturbed.** Level probes and temperature probes with deposit on them behave differently once cleaned, sometimes revealing that a reading you had trusted for years was compensating for fouling.

> Never descale a commercial machine without telling the owner, in advance and in writing, that a leak or a jammed valve may appear as a direct result, and that this indicates a pre-existing defect. That conversation is the difference between a professional job and an argument.

## Check the manufacturer first

Some manufacturers specify a descaling procedure and a product. Some prohibit acid descaling outright, and doing it will void warranty.

Read the documentation for the specific machine before proposing anything. And never use a domestic kettle descaler or an industrial acid — concentration and inhibitor package both matter, and a product formulated for a plastic kettle is not formulated for brass, copper, stainless and elastomer in one circuit.

## Assessing how bad it is

You cannot usually see into the boiler, but scale reports itself in several accessible places. Work through them before deciding anything:

- **The dispersion block passages.** Take one off. Scale here means scale everywhere.
- **Group flow rates**, compared across groups and against the baseline. Restriction indicates deposit in small orifices.
- **The level probe.** Pull it. Scale on the tip and tracking on the insulator is a direct sample of what the boiler interior looks like.
- **Element insulation resistance and heating performance.** A scaled element heats slowly and runs hot internally.
- **The sight glass and its fittings**, where fitted.
- **A borescope through an inspection port or a removed fitting**, if the machine allows it. This is the only way to actually see the boiler, and it is worth the effort on a machine whose fate you are deciding.

## The decision

With that evidence, there are three honest options.

**Light deposit, machine otherwise sound.** Clean the accessible components mechanically — dispersion blocks, gicleurs, probes, screens. Fix the water. Do not put acid through it. Most machines described as "needing a descale" are actually in this category, and mechanical cleaning of accessible parts plus corrected treatment resolves the symptoms.

**Significant deposit, machine has real value and life left.** Strip it. Boiler out, cleaned or descaled out of the machine where you can see and control what is happening, valves and small orifices cleaned or replaced on the bench, all gaskets and seals renewed on reassembly. This is a workshop job with a real labour cost, and it is the only approach that does not gamble with fragment migration.

**Heavy deposit on an older machine.** Get the replacement quote and compare honestly. A full strip and remediation on a machine near the end of its life is money spent to reach a compromised outcome. Say so — clients respect that more than an optimistic repair that fails in six months.

## If a descale does go ahead

- Manufacturer-approved product and concentration only.
- Remove or protect what you can: probes, and anything with a small orifice.
- Do not exceed the specified dwell time. Longer is not more thorough, it is more corrosive.
- Flush extensively afterwards, far more than feels necessary, and confirm the rinse water is neutral.
- Renew group gaskets and any seals that were exposed.
- Clean or replace gicleurs and check every solenoid for trapped fragments.
- Re-test everything: flow rate per group, brew pressure under flow, element insulation resistance, autofill behaviour, and every joint for leaks — cold, then hot.
- Expect to return. Fragments dislodged during the work can take days of normal operation to reach somewhere they cause a problem.

## The argument to make instead

The best version of this conversation happens years earlier, and it is short.

A treatment package costs a fraction of one heat exchanger, and considerably less than a full strip and remediation. Filter cartridges on schedule cost less again. Meanwhile scale damage is plainly visible to anyone who opens the machine, and manufacturers routinely decline warranty claims on it.

Descaling exists because that argument was not made, or not accepted. It is a rescue operation with its own risks, and the way to have a good relationship with it is to never need it.
