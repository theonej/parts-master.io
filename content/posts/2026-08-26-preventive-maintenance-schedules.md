Every task described this month works. What fails is the *system* — the thing that makes them happen after the enthusiasm of the first fortnight has worn off and the person who cared has moved on.

A maintenance schedule is not a list of good intentions. It has four properties: specific numbers, a named owner per task, intervals driven by measurement where possible, and a log that produces trend data.

## Numbers, not adjectives

"Clean the grinder regularly" decays within a month. "Backflush the machine" gets interpreted as three rinse cycles instead of ten.

Every instruction should be executable without judgement by someone on their second week:

- Not "purge the grinder" — **"discard the first 15 g"**.
- Not "backflush with cleaner" — **"¼ tsp, 6 × (5 s on / 10 s off), then 10 rinse cycles"**.
- Not "check the dose" — **"weigh 3 doses at open; target 18.0 g ±0.3 g"**.

Numbers survive turnover. Adjectives do not.

## Intervals from measurement, not the calendar

Some intervals genuinely are time-based. The important ones are not, and treating them as time-based is how bars end up with worn burrs and exhausted cartridges.

| Item | Driven by | Not by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Filter cartridge | Litres, against its rating | "About every six months" |
| Burrs | Kilograms through the grinder | Years since install |
| Baskets | Volume plus reference comparison | Whether they look worn |
| Group gaskets | Handle position | A fixed date |
| Water re-test | Every 6 months, plus after any supply change | Never |

Two of these need a counter. Bean purchases give you kilograms per grinder essentially free. Cartridge volume needs either a water meter — cheap, and worth fitting — or an estimate from drinks sold. Either beats guessing.

The low-volume exception: equipment that is barely used still ages. A decaf grinder doing 200 g a day needs a calendar-based clean, because its throughput will never trigger a usage-based one.

## The schedule

Pulling the month together. Adjust the intervals to volume — a bar doing 15 kg a day belongs at the aggressive end of every range.

**Daily, at close**

- Backflush each group: water, then detergent per the written dose, then the full rinse count.
- Brush group heads and gasket rims.
- Soak baskets and blind baskets; rinse portafilters, handles out of solution.
- Steam wands: purge, wipe with the dedicated cloth, purge again. Dedicated cloth into sanitiser.
- Hot-water flush the drain.
- Wipe down grinders and the surrounding area.
- Empty and clean the drain box strainer.

**Daily, at open**

- Purge each grinder — the stated gram figure, discarded.
- Weigh doses against target; record.
- Check steam pressure and brew temperature against the baseline figures.
- Flush each group.

**Weekly**

- Remove dispersion screens: soak, brush, check against the light, check flatness.
- Remove grinder hoppers: empty, wash, dry completely; brush the bean gate and chamber.
- Grinder cleaning tablets, followed by a sacrificial purge.
- Enzymatic drain treatment, left overnight.
- Remove and soak steam tips; check every hole against the light.

**Monthly**

- Inspect basket rims and holes against the sealed reference basket.
- Check portafilter springs and lugs for play.
- Check group gasket handle position against the reference mark.
- Full grinder chamber and chute clean.
- Clean refrigeration condenser coils.
- Read the water meter; calculate cartridge life remaining.

**Quarterly**

- Remove and clean dispersion blocks, including internal passages.
- Grinder burr strip: clean chamber, chute, carrier; inspect burrs; log kilograms.
- Verify brew pressure under flow at every group.
- Measure and compare group flow rates.
- Test treated water: alkalinity, hardness, chlorine.

**Annually — technician visit**

- Element resistance and insulation to earth, cold and hot, recorded per element.
- All electrical terminals checked for tightness; earth continuity verified.
- Contactors and SSRs inspected; SSR heatsink mounting confirmed.
- Full safety check per yesterday's list, including confirming nothing is bypassed.
- Level probe removed, inspected, cleaned or replaced.
- Pump inlet strainer cleaned; brew pressure recorded.
- Group gaskets, screens and screws replaced as a set.
- Steam valve seals inspected or rebuilt.
- Baskets replaced.
- Vacuum breaker, safety valve and expansion valve checked.
- Temperature offset re-measured; load-recovery test repeated.
- Every reading written into the log against last year's.

## Owners, not "the team"

A task assigned to everybody is assigned to nobody. Each line gets a role — closing barista, shift lead, store manager — and the checklist gets initialled.

The two things that make this work in practice: the checklist lives where the task happens, not in an office folder, and someone senior actually reads the completed sheets. A checklist nobody reviews stops being filled in honestly within a month, and then you have paperwork instead of maintenance.

## The log is the asset

Individual readings are nearly worthless. The series is what has value.

- Element insulation resistance falling year on year tells you which winter it will fail. One reading tells you nothing.
- Brew pressure drifting down over three years is pump wear becoming plannable.
- Group flow rates declining together is scale, caught early.
- Dose spread widening is a grinder problem before anyone tastes it.

The log also does two commercial jobs. It supports warranty claims, because a manufacturer declining a scale-damage claim is answered by treated-water test records. And it demonstrably raises resale value — a machine with a full service history sells, and one without gets a discount.

Paper or digital, whichever actually gets completed. A laminated sheet with a pen on a string outperforms an app nobody opens.

## Review it against reality

Once a year, put the schedule next to the actual fault history. Two questions:

**What failed that the schedule should have caught?** That interval is too long, or the task is missing.

**What did we do repeatedly that never found anything?** That interval can relax.

A schedule that never changes was designed by someone guessing. After two years of your own fault history, it should look meaningfully different from the generic version above — and it will be worth considerably more.

## The economics

Reactive maintenance is not cheaper, it is deferred and then paid at a premium: emergency callout rates, expedited parts, a group or a whole machine out of service during trading, and the coffee sold in the weeks beforehand that was quietly getting worse.

The preventive version is the same work, scheduled, at standard rates, on a Tuesday morning when the bar is quiet. That is the whole argument, and it is the one worth making to an owner who thinks a service contract is an expense rather than a hedge.
