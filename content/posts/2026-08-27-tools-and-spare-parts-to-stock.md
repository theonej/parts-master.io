Almost every diagnostic in this month's posts depends on being able to measure something. A bar without a pressure gauge and a water test kit cannot answer basic questions about its own equipment, and so it guesses — usually by adjusting the grinder.

The kit below is not expensive. It costs less than one emergency callout, and it is what turns "the coffee tastes off" into a specific finding.

## Tools the bar should own

**Measurement — the important half**

- **Scales, 0.1 g resolution.** Dose in, beverage out. The single most useful object on the counter.
- **A timer**, or scales with one built in.
- **A portafilter pressure gauge.** The only way to set or verify brew pressure under flow. Every bar should have one; almost none do.
- **A water test kit** — drop-count titration for alkalinity and hardness, plus chlorine. A TDS pen is fine as a supplementary check, provided everybody understands it cannot measure alkalinity.
- **A jug and the willingness to time a 30-second group flow test.**
- **A thermometer** for milk, and ideally a group thermometer if the bar is serious about temperature.
- **A spirit level.** For the machine, on installation and whenever channelling gets strange.
- **A reference basket**, new, sealed in a bag, never used in service. The comparator for every basket-wear question.
- **A reference burr set**, likewise — it doubles as the spare.

**Cleaning**

- Group brush, and a chute brush for grinders.
- A blind basket per group.
- Espresso machine detergent, and a **measuring spoon** so the dose is a quantity rather than a guess.
- Milk-line cleaner for wands and tips.
- Grinder cleaning granules, if the manufacturer approves them.
- Enzymatic drain treatment.
- Tip-cleaning pins — finer than the holes.
- Dedicated colour-coded cloths: wands separate from counters, no exceptions.

**Basic mechanical**

- A gasket removal hook. Prevents the screwdriver-in-the-group-seat disaster.
- Correct screwdrivers for the screen retaining screws — the right size, so heads do not get burred.
- Allen keys, metric, decent quality.
- Adjustable spanner and a small set of open-ended spanners.
- A soft-bristle brush and a small vacuum for chaff.

## Technician tools

Beyond the bar's own kit, for whoever services it:

- **Multimeter**, and an **insulation resistance tester** — element testing is not possible without the latter.
- **Group thermometer** (Scace-type or thermofilter) for offset measurement.
- **Shim stock and a dial indicator** for burr alignment.
- **Feeler gauges.**
- **A borescope.** Cheap now, and the only way to actually see inside a boiler before deciding a machine's fate.
- **A torque driver** for burr fixings and terminals.
- **A water meter** to fit, so cartridge life is measured rather than estimated.

## Spares worth holding

The test is simple: **would being without this part on a Saturday take a group or the machine out of service?** If yes, and the part is cheap, stock it.

| Part | Quantity | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Group gaskets | One set per group, plus spares | Fails predictably; a group is down without one |
| Dispersion screens + screws | One set per group | Cheap, and screws get burred |
| Baskets | A working set plus the sealed reference | Consumables |
| Blind basket | One per group | Lost or warped surprisingly often |
| Gicleurs | One set | Tiny, cheap, blocks |
| Steam tips | Two | Blocked or damaged threads |
| Steam valve rebuild kit | One per machine | Seals wear; valve body does not |
| Level probe | One | Cheap; failure stops the machine |
| Filter cartridges | One spare, always | Never run past due for lack of stock |
| Burr set | One per grinder | Doubles as wear reference |
| O-ring / seal assortment | One kit | Right size on the day |
| Fuses | Correct ratings | Obvious, always missing |

Every one of those is inexpensive relative to a callout, and each of them has taken a bar out of service in front of me because nobody had one.

## What not to stock

- **Heating elements** — expensive, model-specific, and they generally ship quickly. Unless the site is single-machine with no backup and no tolerance for downtime, they belong at the distributor.
- **Control boards** — expensive, sometimes firmware-specific, and prone to being superseded.
- **Pumps** — moderate cost, usually next-day.
- **Boilers and heat exchangers** — obviously not.

The distinction is whether the part is cheap and consumable, or expensive and rare. Hold the first, order the second.

## Store them properly

Rubber degrades. Gaskets and O-rings left in a hot, bright drawer above a boiler will be hard and cracked when you need them.

- Cool, dark, dry. Not on top of the machine, not in the counter void next to the boiler.
- Labelled with part number and the machine it fits — a drawer of unidentified black rings is not a spares holding.
- Dated on arrival, and rotated. Fit the older gasket first.
- Burrs in their packaging, dry, oiled if the manufacturer supplies them that way.
- One labelled box or drawer, with a list on the lid, and someone responsible for reordering what gets used.

That last point is what makes the difference. A spares holding without a reorder trigger becomes an empty box within a year, and nobody notices until the morning they need something.

## Closing the month

Thirty-two posts, and the through-line has been consistent: **measure it, write it down, and fix the cause rather than the symptom.**

Water treatment, specified from a real test rather than assumed. Electrical supply established before the machine is chosen. Drainage with fall and an air gap. A heat load someone actually calculated. Cutouts that let the machine be serviced. A baseline recorded at commissioning so that every later question is a comparison. Consumables replaced on measured intervals. Diagnostics that distinguish a hardware fault from a technique problem before a team gets retrained for something they cannot fix.

None of it is difficult. Most of it is cheap. Nearly all of it happens at a moment when it feels optional — during a fit-out when budgets are tight, or at close when everyone is tired.

The bars whose machines reach year ten looking after their tenth-year coffee are the ones that treated those moments as the job rather than the overhead. That is the whole trade, and the water test is still where it starts.
