Installation manuals get skimmed for the dimensions and then set aside. Everything that matters is in the parts that get skipped.

They are also badly written, often translated, and organised for the manufacturer's convenience rather than the installer's. That is not a reason to ignore them — it is a reason to read them deliberately, with a highlighter, before quoting.

## Get the right document

There are usually three, and people work from the wrong one.

- **The brochure** gives body dimensions and marketing figures. Useless for installation.
- **The installation manual** gives service clearances, supply requirements and commissioning procedure. This is the one you need.
- **The service manual** gives exploded diagrams, part numbers and torque figures. Often only available to authorised technicians — ask for it explicitly when you buy.

Ask the distributor for the installation and service manuals at order time, not on installation day. On some brands the service documentation is gated, and that gate is worth knowing about before you commit a client to the machine.

## What to extract before quoting

Work through with a pen and pull out these specifics:

- **Service clearances**, not body dimensions. Rear, side and above. This drives counter depth and it is the number cabinetmakers need.
- **Electrical supply**: voltage, phase, total load, and whether the element configuration is factory-set. Also whether the machine wants a neutral on three-phase.
- **Water supply**: inlet pressure range, minimum flow rate, connection size and thread standard.
- **The water quality specification.** Read this twice — see below.
- **Drain requirements**: connection size, and whether the manufacturer specifies an air gap.
- **Weight, dry and filled.**
- **Ambient limits.** Many manuals give a maximum ambient operating temperature that a poorly ventilated counter void will exceed.
- **Commissioning procedure**, including any first-fill sequence and any run-in requirement.
- **Torque figures**, if given, for element terminals and boiler fittings.

## The water specification is a warranty condition

This is the most commercially important paragraph in the document and it is usually a small table nobody reads.

Manufacturers specify hardness, alkalinity, chloride and chlorine limits. Those figures are not advice. They are the conditions under which the warranty applies, and scale damage is plainly visible to anyone who opens a machine.

Two practical consequences:

1. **Specify treatment to the manufacturer's numbers, not to a generic standard**, where the two differ. Then record the treated-water test results at commissioning, dated. That record is what answers a declined claim.
2. **If the manufacturer's figures conflict with what you would otherwise specify**, follow the manufacturer's and note the difference. Being right about water chemistry does not help a client whose claim has been refused.

> Photograph or extract the water specification page and put it in the handover file, next to the treated water test results. Those two documents together are the warranty defence.

## Also read the warranty conditions themselves

Usually a page near the back, in small type. Things that routinely appear:

- Installation by an authorised party as a condition of cover.
- Water treatment requirements, as above.
- Commissioning documentation required for cover to start.
- Registration within a stated period.
- Exclusions for scale, corrosion, incorrect supply voltage and unauthorised modification.

Read them before the machine is installed, because several of them are conditions you can only satisfy at installation time.

## What manuals leave out

Knowing the gaps matters as much as reading the content.

- **Real service access.** A manual specifies rear clearance for airflow, not the space a hand needs to reach a union. Add your own margin.
- **Filter and treatment plumbing.** Usually treated as somebody else's problem, so the manual will not tell you the cartridge needs swing clearance.
- **Anything about the counter.** Load, cutout tolerances and fixing are all outside the manufacturer's scope.
- **Realistic warm-up time.** Stated figures are often for the boiler reaching setpoint, not for the group mass reaching equilibrium.
- **Local code.** Backflow prevention, drainage and electrical protection are jurisdictional and the manual will not cover yours.

## Translation and units

Manuals translated from another language routinely have ambiguous or inverted instructions, and mixed units. Two habits worth keeping:

- **Check units on every figure** — bar against psi, and litres against gallons. A pressure figure that seems implausible usually is, and it is usually a unit error in the document.
- **Where an instruction is ambiguous, ask rather than infer.** A short email to technical support beats a confident guess about which direction a bypass screw increases pressure.

If you find a genuine error, tell the distributor. It gets fixed, and it marks you as someone who read it.

## Keep it accessible

The manual belongs in the handover file and a copy should stay with the machine — not in an office two floors up.

Also keep your own library, organised by manufacturer and model, digital and searchable. The second time you meet a model, having your own annotated copy with the figures you extracted last time turns an unfamiliar machine into a known one.
