A machine's serviceability is decided by its supply chain, not by its engineering. Two well-built machines can have completely different ownership experiences because one has parts on a shelf in your country and the other has a six-week wait.

That is a question to answer before specifying, and clients almost never think to ask it.

## Ask these before committing a client to a brand

- **Where are parts held, and what is the realistic lead time** for a group gasket, a solenoid coil, an element and a control board? Ask for all four — the answer differs enormously across that range.
- **How long are parts supported after a model is discontinued?** Some manufacturers commit to a period. Others do not, and a machine can become unserviceable while it is still physically sound.
- **Is service documentation available**, and to whom? Gated service manuals are common. Find out whether you will be given diagrams and part numbers, or left to guess.
- **Is there technical support, in what hours, and staffed by whom?** A support line answered by an engineer is worth a great deal on a Saturday. One answered by a sales team is not support.
- **Is training available**, and does it come with parts access?
- **Are there authorised-installer conditions** on the warranty?

A distributor who answers those clearly is telling you they have thought about the machine's whole life. One who deflects is telling you something too.

## Lead time beats unit price

A group gasket that costs slightly more but is on a shelf two hours away is worth more than a cheaper one on a container. The comparison that matters is not part cost — it is **cost of the part plus cost of the downtime while you wait for it.**

A group out of service through a weekend on a busy bar costs more than the entire annual parts spend. Which is the whole argument for the spares kit: the cheap, predictable consumables belong on the client's shelf so that lead time never enters the picture for them.

> Ask for the lead time on the ten cheapest parts, not the expensive ones. Bars go down over gaskets, coils, screens and cartridges — not over boilers.

## Genuine, equivalent and neither

Three categories, and being honest about them matters.

**Genuine.** Manufacturer-supplied. Correct by definition, warranty-safe, usually most expensive. Non-negotiable for anything safety-related: safety valves, thermal protection, elements, pressure components.

**Equivalent.** Made to the same specification by a reputable supplier — many gaskets, seals, screens, common valves and standard fittings are commodity items. Often good, sometimes better stocked, and fine for non-safety consumables provided you know the actual specification you are matching.

**Neither.** Approximately similar parts sold as fitting. A gasket in the wrong thickness changes where the portafilter locks. A slightly different gicleur changes the machine's character. Save money here and you will be back.

The rule I hold to: genuine for anything that contains pressure or interrupts a fault, equivalent is acceptable for wear items where the specification is known and verifiable, never approximate.

## Single-source risk

If one distributor is the only route to parts, documentation and support for a machine, that is a business risk for both you and your client. Distributors change hands, lose agencies and go under.

Worth knowing:

- Whether the manufacturer has other routes into your market.
- Whether parts are available direct in an emergency.
- Whether the machine uses common components — many use industry-standard pumps, solenoids, elements and fittings, which makes them far easier to keep alive.

A machine built largely from standard components is more serviceable in year twelve than one built entirely from proprietary parts, regardless of which was better engineered. That is worth weighing at specification time.

## Build the relationship deliberately

Distributor relationships are a technical asset, and they are built before you need them.

- **Register properly** as a service partner where such schemes exist. It usually unlocks documentation and pricing.
- **Take the training.** Even when you know the machine, the factory course gets you the direct contacts and the parts access.
- **Report faults you find**, particularly patterns across several machines. Manufacturers act on that, and it makes you a known quantity.
- **Get a named technical contact** rather than a general address.
- **Pay on time.** The engineer who gets a part released on a Friday afternoon is the one whose account is clean.

## Obsolescence planning

Every machine eventually becomes unserviceable. The useful thing is knowing roughly when.

For a client with a fleet, that means asset lifecycle planning: knowing which machines are approaching the end of parts support and budgeting replacement rather than discovering it when a board fails.

For a single-site client, it means a conversation at around year eight about whether the next major failure is worth repairing. That conversation goes far better in advance than it does on the morning the machine stops.

Keep a note of discontinuation announcements for the models you service. It is the kind of thing that seems irrelevant until a control board is unavailable and a client is asking why nobody warned them.
