Every utility publishes a water quality report, and it is the first thing people reach for when specifying treatment. It is genuinely useful — for anticipating what you will find, not for specifying anything.

The report exists to demonstrate regulatory compliance. It is not an equipment specification document, and using it as one is how bars end up with treatment sized for water they do not have.

## Four reasons it is not your water

**It is an average.** Usually annual, often across a whole supply zone. Your water varies seasonally, and the report has smoothed that out. A zone averaging 180 mg/L hardness can run considerably higher in late summer.

**It is sampled upstream of you.** At treatment works or distribution points, not at your tap. Everything between — mains, service pipe, riser, internal plumbing — can change the result.

**Supply zones blend.** Utilities mix sources and re-balance them through the year. Your street can differ measurably from one a mile away, and both are inside the same reported zone.

**Your building may already be treating it.** In hard-water areas, commercial premises frequently have a softener installed for the boiler or dishwasher, and the coffee bar gets fed from downstream of it without anyone mentioning it. Storage tanks are the other common surprise: stagnation, warming, and sometimes contamination.

> Use the report to predict what you will find and to decide which tests to run. Then test at the tap the machine will connect to. The report tells you what to expect; only a sample tells you what you have.

## What the report does tell you usefully

**Regional character.** Whether you are in a hard or soft area, and roughly how hard. That determines whether you are having a small conversation about carbon filtration or a large one about dealkalisation.

**Disinfectant type.** This one is genuinely important and hard to determine any other way. Free chlorine and chloramine need different media and different contact times. The report will say which is used, and utilities sometimes switch seasonally — which is worth knowing, because a carbon filter sized for chlorine will let chloramine through.

**Source type.** Groundwater is typically harder, more consistent and higher in alkalinity. Surface water is softer, more variable, and more likely to carry organics. This tells you how much seasonal drift to expect.

**Whether they report alkalinity at all.** Many do not, which is itself informative — it means the number you most need is the one you will definitely have to measure.

## Finding the numbers, through the units

Reports use whichever units the local convention favours, and hardness gets expressed at least five ways. Conversions to mg/L as CaCO₃:

| Unit | Multiply by |
| --- | --- |
| Grains per gallon (US) | 17.1 |
| German degrees (°dH) | 17.85 |
| French degrees (°fH) | 10.0 |
| Clark degrees (°e) | 14.3 |
| mg/L as Ca (calcium ion) | 2.5 |

That last row catches people. A report giving "calcium: 80 mg/L" is reporting the calcium ion, which is 200 mg/L as CaCO₃ — a very different-looking number for the same water.

What to look for, in order of usefulness: total or calcium hardness, alkalinity (if given), chloride, disinfectant type and residual, pH, sodium, and sulphate.

## Chloride deserves a specific look

Reports usually do give chloride, and it is the one figure people skip. It is not removed by softening or by a carbon block, it causes pitting corrosion in stainless steel, and coastal supplies can run high.

If the report shows chloride above about 50 mg/L, that shapes the treatment decision before you have taken a sample — it pushes you toward reverse osmosis rather than a cartridge.

## How to actually use it

1. Download the report for your specific supply zone, not the utility-wide summary.
2. Convert everything to mg/L as CaCO₃ so you are comparing like with like.
3. Note the disinfectant type, and whether it changes seasonally.
4. Note chloride, and whether the source is coastal or affected by road salt.
5. Use all of that to decide which tests to run and roughly what treatment to price.
6. **Then sample and test at the tap**, and specify from the measured numbers.

The report is reconnaissance. It stops you arriving with the wrong test kit and lets you give a client a realistic budget range before you have been on site. It just does not get to set the specification.
