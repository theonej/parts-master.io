A water specification rests entirely on the sample it was derived from. Get the sample wrong and every downstream decision inherits the error — with the confidence of a measured number attached.

The good news is that a correct sample takes five minutes and no special equipment.

## Sample from the right tap

The tap the machine will actually connect to. Not the kitchen sink, not the customer toilet, not a hose bib outside.

This matters more than it sounds. Buildings contain softeners, storage tanks, filtered drinking taps and dead legs, and different taps can be fed differently. Two specific traps:

- **An existing filtered or softened outlet.** You measure lovely water, specify minimal treatment, and the machine gets plumbed into the unsoftened main.
- **A tap downstream of a building softener** that nobody mentioned. Sodium will be high and hardness near zero — which should make you suspicious rather than pleased.

If you cannot be certain what a tap is fed from, trace it or ask the building's maintenance contact.

## Flush first

Water standing in a pipe is not the water the machine will receive in service. It has been in contact with pipe walls for hours, it has warmed up, and its chlorine has partly dissipated.

**Run the tap for two to five minutes** until the temperature stabilises. That is your service sample.

Then, if you want the extra information, take a **second, first-draw sample** on another visit — filling immediately without flushing. Comparing the two tells you what the building's own plumbing is contributing, which is where dissolved copper, lead and zinc show up. That is a plumbing question rather than a coffee one, but it occasionally matters and it is nearly free to gather.

## Containers and headspace

- **Clean and inert.** A new sample bottle, or a clean glass or food-grade plastic bottle. Never a container that held anything else — a rinsed juice bottle will skew results.
- **Rinse three times with the sample water** before filling. This conditions the container and removes anything from it.
- **Fill to the brim with no headspace**, and cap tightly. Trapped air lets CO₂ exchange with the water, which shifts pH and alkalinity readings. This is the most common avoidable error.
- **Do not touch the inside** of the cap or bottle.

## Test chlorine on site, immediately

Free chlorine dissipates. By the time a sample reaches a bench an hour later, the reading is meaningless and usually reassuringly low.

Chlorine and chloramine are on-site tests, taken at the tap, within moments of filling. If your treatment decision depends on the disinfectant type — and it does, since chloramine needs catalytic carbon — this is not a test you can defer.

pH is in the same category. It drifts as CO₂ exchanges, so a same-day reading is far more trustworthy than one taken the next morning.

> Alkalinity, hardness and chlorine are the three numbers your specification turns on, and all three are best measured on site with a drop kit. If you only carry one thing, carry the titration kit.

## Label it, properly

Every sample gets: date, time, exact location, flush duration, whether it was first-draw or flushed, water temperature if known, and who took it.

This feels excessive until you are holding three unlabelled bottles from two sites. It also matters for the record — a treated-water result in a warranty file is worth much more when it says where and when it was taken.

## Sampling treated water

Same discipline, one addition: sample **after** the cartridge has been flushed for its full run-in volume. A new cartridge that has not been flushed gives readings that reflect the manufacturing residue rather than the media's steady-state performance.

Take treated samples at the machine inlet, which is the only place the number actually means anything. A sample taken at the filter head before a length of pipe and a blend tee is not what the machine drinks.

## If you are sending it to a lab

Worth doing once at specification time for a full ion breakdown — chloride, sulphate, silica, sodium, and the metals.

Call them first. Labs specify their own containers, volumes and preservatives, and some analyses need acid-preserved bottles or refrigeration and have short holding times. A sample taken in your own bottle and posted on a Friday may be rejected or, worse, analysed and reported inaccurately.

## The checklist

1. Confirm the tap is the one the machine will use, and what feeds it.
2. Flush 2–5 minutes until temperature stabilises.
3. Rinse container three times with sample water.
4. Fill to the brim, no headspace, cap tight.
5. Test chlorine and pH on site, now.
6. Titrate alkalinity and hardness on site or same day.
7. Label fully.
8. Record the results with the date, in the water log.
