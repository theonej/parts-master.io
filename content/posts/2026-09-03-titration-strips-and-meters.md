Four ways to measure water, each answering a different question. Most bars own the one that answers the least useful question and none of the others.

## Conductivity meters — the TDS pen

**Measures:** electrical conductivity, converted to an estimated total dissolved solids figure by a fixed multiplier.

**Tells you:** roughly how much dissolved ionic material is present, in total.

**Cannot tell you:** which ions. This is the whole limitation. Two waters reading 150 ppm can be mostly calcium bicarbonate — scaling, buffering, flattening — or mostly sodium chloride, which will not scale and will pit stainless. Identical reading, opposite problems.

**It also cannot measure alkalinity**, which is the number your treatment specification turns on.

**Legitimate uses:** confirming an RO membrane is working, spotting a gross change from a known baseline, checking a blend has moved. All of those are *comparisons against a known reference*, which is what the instrument is actually good for.

The TDS pen is not useless. It is over-trusted, because it is cheap and gives an instant number that feels like an answer.

## Test strips

**Measures:** a colour change compared against a printed chart.

**Good for:** a fast go/no-go on chlorine. Is there any, or is there none. That is a genuinely useful question and strips answer it adequately.

**Poor for:** anything needing resolution. Hardness and alkalinity strips typically resolve in coarse bands, and interpretation is subjective under bar lighting. You cannot set a blend valve from a strip.

**They also degrade.** Strips absorb moisture and lose sensitivity, particularly in a humid coffee bar, and an out-of-date pot reads low. Note the opening date and replace them.

## Drop titration — the workhorse

**Measures:** alkalinity (carbonate hardness) and total hardness, by counting reagent drops until an endpoint colour change.

This is the instrument to own. Cheap, robust, no calibration, and it directly measures the two numbers that decide your treatment. An aquarium KH/GH kit works perfectly well.

**Doing it properly:**

- Use the exact sample volume the kit specifies. Halving the volume doubles your error per drop.
- Hold the bottle vertically so drop size is consistent. Angled bottles give small drops and a low reading.
- Swirl between drops, not after several.
- Count out loud or mark them. Losing count is the main error source.
- Watch for the *first* persistent colour change, not the fully developed colour.
- Run it twice. Two readings that agree are worth far more than one.

**Mind the units.** Many kits report in German degrees. One °dKH is 17.85 mg/L as CaCO₃, so a reading of 4 drops is about 71 mg/L, not 4. Getting this wrong by a factor of eighteen is a common and consequential mistake.

**Reagents expire.** Date them on opening and replace annually.

> If you own one water instrument, own a drop titration kit. It measures alkalinity, which no meter in this list can do, and alkalinity is what decides scale and buffering.

## Digital titrators and photometers

**Digital titrators** do the same job as drop kits with better resolution and a higher price. Worth it if you are specifying treatment regularly and want repeatability you can defend.

**Photometers** read a colour change instrumentally rather than by eye, removing the subjectivity. Genuinely better for chlorine and for low-level readings, and the sensible upgrade if strips are not resolving enough.

Both are conveniences rather than capability changes. A careful drop titration gets you most of the way.

## Full lab analysis

**Measures:** individual ions — chloride, sulphate, sodium, silica, calcium, magnesium, plus metals.

This is the only way to see chloride, which nobody can measure in the field and which determines whether you can use a cartridge at all or need RO. It also catches sulphate and silica, and it gives you sodium so you can tell whether you are already receiving softened water.

**Do it once, at specification time, on every new site.** It is not expensive relative to the treatment it is specifying, and it is the difference between a designed system and a guess. Then use field titration for ongoing verification.

## What to own

| Instrument | Own it? | For |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drop titration kit | Yes | Alkalinity and hardness — the specification numbers |
| Chlorine test (strip or photometer) | Yes | Disinfectant present or absent, on site |
| TDS pen | Yes, with caveats | Comparison against a known baseline |
| Lab analysis | Once per site | Chloride, sodium, sulphate, silica |
| Digital titrator / photometer | Optional | Repeatability if you specify often |

The pattern: **measure alkalinity in the field, send chloride to a lab, and never let a conductivity reading stand in for either.**
