If there is one conceptual error that causes more bad water decisions than any other, it is treating hardness and alkalinity as the same thing, or as reliably proportional. They are neither.

## The definitions, precisely

**Total hardness** is the concentration of divalent cations — in practice calcium and magnesium. It is what makes water "hard" in the domestic sense: limescale in a kettle, poor lathering.

**Calcium hardness** is the calcium portion alone. **Magnesium hardness** is the rest.

**Total alkalinity** is something different in kind: it is the water's *capacity to neutralise acid*. In drinking water it is almost entirely bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻), with small carbonate and hydroxide contributions.

So hardness counts cations. Alkalinity measures anion buffering capacity. They are two independent properties, and water can have any combination of them.

## They genuinely move independently

Two waters that make the point:

- **High hardness, low alkalinity.** Water dominated by calcium sulphate — gypsum. Plenty of calcium, little buffering. It will not scale heavily but it is unbuffered.
- **Low hardness, high alkalinity.** Water dominated by sodium bicarbonate. Almost no calcium, substantial buffering. It will flatten your coffee thoroughly while barely scaling at all.

Most municipal supplies sit between, with hardness and alkalinity roughly comparable, which is why the two get conflated. But "roughly comparable in typical supplies" is not "the same thing", and treatment behaves very differently depending on which one you are moving.

## Temporary and permanent hardness

The bridge between the two concepts.

**Temporary (carbonate) hardness** is the portion of hardness paired with bicarbonate. Heat it and it precipitates as calcium carbonate — this is scale. It is "temporary" because boiling removes it.

**Permanent (non-carbonate) hardness** is the portion paired with sulphate or chloride. It does not precipitate on heating. It stays in solution, contributes to mouthfeel, and does not scale.

Temporary hardness is limited by whichever is lower — hardness or alkalinity. This is the practical insight: **reducing alkalinity reduces scale potential even if calcium stays the same**, because there is no longer enough bicarbonate to pair with the calcium.

That is the entire principle behind dealkalisation, and the reason it is the correct treatment for espresso while softening is not.

## Which number causes which problem

| Property | Consequence |
| --- | --- |
| Alkalinity (high) | Scale formation; buffers coffee acidity → flat, dull, chalky cup |
| Alkalinity (very low) | No buffering; pH instability; corrosive to copper and brass |
| Calcium + magnesium | Binds flavour compounds; contributes body and mouthfeel |
| Calcium (very low) | Thin, hollow, papery espresso |
| Total hardness (high, non-carbonate) | Mouthfeel, some deposit, but not aggressive scaling |

Read that table and the softener problem becomes obvious. Ion exchange removes calcium and magnesium and leaves alkalinity untouched. You lose the row that gives body, keep the row that flattens acidity, and add sodium. Scale stops; the coffee gets worse.

> Scale is an alkalinity problem far more than a hardness problem. Treat the alkalinity and you address both scale and flatness. Treat only the hardness and you address neither taste problem.

## Units, and the conversion that catches people

Everything should be expressed as **mg/L as CaCO₃** so the numbers are comparable. Converting:

| From | Multiply by |
| --- | --- |
| Grains per gallon (US) | 17.1 |
| German degrees (°dH, °dKH) | 17.85 |
| French degrees (°fH) | 10.0 |
| Clark degrees (°e) | 14.3 |
| mg/L as Ca²⁺ (the ion) | 2.5 |
| mg/L as Mg²⁺ (the ion) | 4.1 |

The last two are where reports mislead. "Calcium 80 mg/L" means 200 mg/L as CaCO₃. A moderately hard water can look soft if you read the ion figure as though it were the CaCO₃ figure.

## What to measure and aim for

Measure both, always, with a titration kit — a GH test for total hardness and a KH test for alkalinity. Two tests, five minutes.

Working targets for espresso, tighter than the SCA brew standard because a pressurised boiler is less forgiving:

- **Alkalinity: 30–50 mg/L as CaCO₃.** The number to control.
- **Calcium hardness: 50–75 mg/L as CaCO₃.** Enough for body, not enough to be a problem once alkalinity is handled.

Two levers, set independently. That framing is what makes the rest of this month's treatment decisions straightforward — you are choosing a technology based on which lever it moves.
