Both must reach the machine at zero. They are not the same problem, and the filter that handles one comfortably can let the other through.

This is the most common quiet failure in café water treatment: a carbon cartridge correctly fitted, a chloraminated supply, and a persistent swimming-pool note in the coffee that nobody can place.

## Free chlorine

Chlorine dosed into water forms hypochlorous acid and hypochlorite. It is highly reactive, which makes it an effective disinfectant and also means it dissipates — which is why it must be tested at the tap rather than back at the bench.

**Removal is easy.** Activated carbon removes free chlorine by a *catalytic reduction* reaction at the carbon surface, not by adsorption. It is fast and needs relatively little contact time, and standard granular carbon does it well.

## Chloramine

Chloramine is chlorine combined with ammonia, usually as monochloramine. Utilities have moved toward it precisely because it is **more stable** — it persists through long distribution networks and holds a residual at the far end where free chlorine would have decayed.

That stability is exactly what makes it hard to remove. Chloramine reacts with carbon far more slowly than free chlorine, so:

- **Standard granular carbon removes it slowly and incompletely.** A cartridge sized on free-chlorine assumptions will pass chloramine at peak flow.
- **It needs catalytic carbon** — carbon whose surface has been modified to accelerate the reaction.
- **It needs substantially more contact time**, commonly cited as two to three times that for free chlorine.

Chloramine also breaks down releasing ammonia, and is generally harder on elastomers than free chlorine at equivalent residual.

The taste it leaves is distinctive once you know it: medicinal, faintly chemical, sometimes described as chlorophenol. It sits underneath everything and survives every recipe change.

## Establishing which you have

Two routes, and use both.

**The utility report** states the disinfectant. Note that some utilities switch seasonally — free chlorine at one time of year, chloramine at another — which means a system designed for the easier case fails for part of the year.

**A test kit that measures free and total chlorine.** This is the definitive on-site method and it is simple:

- **Free chlorine** is the uncombined residual.
- **Total chlorine** is free plus combined.
- **Total minus free = combined chlorine, which is your chloramine.**

So a reading of, say, 0.1 mg/L free and 1.2 mg/L total tells you immediately that you are dealing with chloramine and that a standard carbon block is not the answer. A test that only reads free chlorine will show a reassuring low number and tell you nothing.

> If you carry only one chlorine test, carry one that reads **total** chlorine. A free-chlorine-only reading on a chloraminated supply is actively misleading.

## Specifying for it

- **Catalytic carbon**, explicitly. Not "carbon".
- **Size on flow rate, not just capacity.** Contact time is inversely related to flow, so a cartridge that removes chloramine at 2 L/min may not at 6 L/min. The peak simultaneous demand figure is what matters here.
- **Design for the harder case** if the utility switches seasonally.
- **Verify at the machine inlet.** Target zero total chlorine, measured, after the cartridge has been flushed for its full run-in volume.

## Carbon must come before the resin

Worth stating explicitly because getting the order wrong destroys expensive media.

**Chlorine and chloramine oxidise ion exchange resin.** A weak acid cation cartridge or a softener placed upstream of the carbon will have its resin progressively degraded, losing capacity long before its rated volume.

The correct train order is:

1. Sediment prefiltration
2. Carbon — catalytic if chloramine is present
3. Dealkalisation or softening
4. Blending or remineralisation, where applicable

Combination cartridges handle this internally, but if you are assembling a train from separate stages, the sequence is not optional. Reverse osmosis membranes are even less tolerant: thin-film composite membranes are destroyed by chlorine, so the carbon stage ahead of an RO membrane is protecting the most expensive component in the system.

## After a closure

Carbon beds are nutrient-rich surfaces sitting in warm, still water. Over a holiday closure they support bacterial growth.

Flush the carbon stage before service resumes after any extended shutdown. It takes two minutes and it is the difference between the first coffee of the season tasting fine and tasting of the cupboard.
