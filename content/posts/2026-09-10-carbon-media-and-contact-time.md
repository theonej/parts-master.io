Carbon is the one treatment stage almost every bar has, and the one most often specified by size of housing rather than by what it needs to do.

## Two different jobs

Carbon does two unrelated things, and they have different implications for sizing and life.

**Chlorine reduction is catalytic.** The carbon surface catalyses a reduction reaction. It is not consumed in the way adsorption capacity is consumed, so a carbon bed can keep reducing chlorine for a long time — provided the surface stays available and the water is in contact with it long enough.

**Taste, odour and organics removal is adsorption.** Organic molecules stick to the internal pore surface. That capacity is finite and it fills up. Once it is full, organics pass through even though chlorine reduction may still be working.

The practical consequence: a cartridge can be past its useful life for taste while still handling chlorine, which is why "the chlorine test still reads zero" is not a reason to leave a cartridge in beyond its rated volume.

## Contact time is the specification that matters

The governing parameter is **empty bed contact time** — how long the water spends in the carbon bed. It is simply bed volume divided by flow rate.

Double the flow and you halve the contact time. So a cartridge that removes chloramine perfectly at 2 L/min may pass it at 6 L/min, with no change to the cartridge and nothing visibly wrong.

This is why the flow rating on a datasheet is not marketing. **Capacity tells you the change interval; flow rating tells you whether it works during the morning rush.** A bar whose water tastes fine at 10 a.m. and faintly of chlorine at 8:30 a.m. has a contact time problem, not a cartridge problem.

> Size carbon on peak simultaneous flow, then confirm capacity separately. Getting these the wrong way round produces a system that is correctly rated on paper and fails exactly when the bar is busiest.

## The media types

**Granular activated carbon (GAC).** Loose granules. Low pressure drop, high capacity for its volume, inexpensive.

Its weakness is **channelling**: water finds preferential paths through the bed, so some of it gets much less contact than the average suggests. Loose beds can also fluidise and settle over time.

**Carbon block.** Carbon extruded or bonded into a solid porous body. Water is forced through a uniform structure, so contact is far more consistent and channelling is eliminated. It also filters particulates, often to a stated micron rating, so it does double duty.

The trade is a higher pressure drop, which matters when you are stacking stages and working within a machine's inlet pressure requirement.

For a coffee bar, block is usually the better choice: consistent contact matters more than the pressure drop costs you.

**Catalytic carbon.** Carbon whose surface has been modified to accelerate the chloramine reaction specifically. Necessary on chloraminated supplies, and there is no substitute for it — more standard carbon is not equivalent to the right carbon.

**Impregnated and bacteriostatic media.** Silver-impregnated carbon inhibits bacterial growth in the bed. Worth considering on low-volume sites where water sits still for long periods.

## Bacterial growth, and the holiday problem

Carbon is a large, rough, nutrient-bearing surface sitting in dechlorinated water at room temperature. It is close to ideal for biofilm.

In continuous service this is largely self-managing, because water moves through constantly. In a bar closed for two weeks it is not. The bed stagnates, warms, and grows.

Two habits:

- **Flush the carbon stage before service resumes** after any extended closure. Several litres to drain, not through the machine.
- **On very low-volume sites, change carbon on a calendar basis** even if the volume calculation says otherwise. Media does not last indefinitely in still water.

## Fines, and why you flush a new cartridge

New carbon sheds fine dust. If the first water through a fresh cartridge goes into the machine, those fines travel to the places you least want them: gicleurs, solenoid seats and flowmeters.

Flush every new cartridge to drain, for the manufacturer's stated volume, before any of it reaches the machine. There is a whole post on this later in the month because it is skipped so routinely.

## Position in the train

Carbon goes **after** sediment prefiltration and **before** any ion exchange or membrane stage.

- Sediment first, so particulates do not blind the carbon.
- Carbon before resin, because chlorine oxidises ion exchange resin and destroys its capacity.
- Carbon before an RO membrane, because chlorine destroys thin-film composite membranes outright.

That last point makes the carbon stage the guardian of the most expensive component in an RO system. Its failure is not a taste problem, it is a membrane replacement.

## Commissioning and verification

1. Confirm the media type matches the disinfectant — catalytic if chloramine.
2. Confirm the flow rating exceeds measured peak simultaneous demand.
3. Flush for the full stated volume, to drain.
4. Measure **total** chlorine at the machine inlet. Target zero.
5. Record pressure drop across the stage as a baseline, so future loading is visible.
6. Log the model, install date and rated volume on the tag at the filter head.
