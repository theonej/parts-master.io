This is the treatment most specialty bars should be using, and the mechanism is worth understanding because it explains both why it works and how it fails.

The commercial products are familiar — the BWT bestmax, Everpure Claris and Brita Purity C families are all built on this chemistry.

## The mechanism

Weak acid cation resin carries carboxylic acid functional groups, and it is supplied in the **hydrogen form** — loaded with H⁺ rather than sodium.

As water passes through, calcium and magnesium bind to the resin and **hydrogen ions** are released. Those hydrogen ions immediately react with the bicarbonate in the water:

**H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃ → CO₂ + H₂O**

The bicarbonate is converted to carbonic acid, which decomposes to carbon dioxide and water.

That is the crucial difference from a softener. A softener *swaps* cations and leaves the bicarbonate intact. WAC resin *consumes* the bicarbonate — the alkalinity is destroyed, not relocated.

## What comes out

- **Hardness reduced.** Calcium and magnesium are exchanged onto the resin.
- **Alkalinity reduced.** Converted to dissolved CO₂.
- **No sodium added.** Nothing is being traded for sodium.
- **pH drops slightly**, and dissolved CO₂ rises.

Both of the numbers that matter move in the right direction with one cartridge. That is why this is the correct tool for espresso and a softener is not.

## Capacity depends on your water

This catches people, and it is why two identical cartridges on two sites have completely different change intervals.

The resin has a fixed number of exchange sites. How much water it can treat before those sites are full depends on how much hardness and alkalinity is in each litre. Harder, more alkaline water exhausts it faster.

Manufacturers publish capacity tables — litres of treated water against feed hardness, usually in German degrees. A cartridge rated 12,000 litres at 5 °dH might manage 4,000 at 15 °dH.

**So you cannot know the change interval without the water test.** A quoted headline capacity figure is meaningless on its own, and a schedule built from "the cartridge does 10,000 litres" without checking which column that came from will be wrong by a factor of two or three.

## It leaks rather than failing cleanly

The most important operational point.

WAC resin does not work perfectly and then stop. As the bed approaches exhaustion, the treated water's alkalinity **rises progressively** — untreated character bleeds through gradually over an extended period.

Consequences:

- **You cannot detect exhaustion by taste** until it is well advanced, and by then the machine has been receiving increasingly alkaline water for weeks.
- **You must change on measured volume**, not on symptoms.
- **Change at around 80–85% of rated capacity**, not at 100%, because the last portion is already degraded output.

This is the single strongest argument for fitting a water meter. Without one you are relying on an estimate to schedule a component whose failure is invisible.

> A WAC cartridge does not tell you when it is finished. It quietly gets worse. Measure the volume, change early, and titrate the output at every service.

## Bypass, and why you want some

Full treatment through the resin would strip hardness and alkalinity too far — thin coffee and unbuffered, mildly aggressive water.

The bypass blends a controlled proportion of untreated feed back into the output, which:

- Brings hardness back up toward the 50–75 mg/L target for body.
- Leaves useful alkalinity, in the 30–50 mg/L range.
- Extends cartridge life, since less water passes through the resin.

Set it from the measured feed water, then **verify by titration**. Detail in a later post; the principle is that the dial position is a starting point and the titration is the answer.

Note the same caveat as RO blending: bypassed water brings chloride and silica through proportionally. On a high-chloride supply, bypass is working against you.

## Carbon must come first

Chlorine and chloramine oxidise ion exchange resin and destroy capacity. The carbon stage ahead of the resin is protecting it.

Most commercial cartridges integrate sediment, carbon and WAC resin in one body with the correct internal sequence. If you are assembling separate stages, the order is not negotiable.

## Where it is the wrong choice

Be clear about the limits:

- **High chloride.** WAC does nothing to chloride. This needs RO.
- **High silica.** Likewise.
- **Very high alkalinity.** Technically it works, but capacity collapses and you are changing cartridges constantly. RO becomes cheaper to run.
- **Already-softened feed.** With almost no hardness present there is little for the resin to exchange against, so alkalinity removal is poor.

## Commissioning and servicing

1. Test the feed water: hardness, alkalinity, chlorine, and chloride from the lab analysis.
2. Read the capacity from the manufacturer's table **at your measured hardness**.
3. Set the bypass from the feed figures.
4. Flush for the full stated run-in volume, to drain.
5. **Titrate the output at the machine inlet.** Confirm 30–50 alkalinity, 50–75 calcium hardness.
6. Record model, install date, meter reading, bypass setting and the test results on the tag and in the log.
7. Re-titrate at every service. Rising alkalinity in the output means exhaustion or a drifted bypass.
