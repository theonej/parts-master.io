Having established that alkalinity is the number to control, the question becomes how. There are fewer real options than the market suggests, and the choice is usually forced by one or two parameters rather than by preference.

## The options

**Weak acid cation cartridge.** Resin in hydrogen form consumes bicarbonate. Compact, no waste water, no power, no plumbing beyond a filter head. Recurring cartridge cost, capacity dependent on feed water, and it does nothing for chloride or silica.

**Reverse osmosis with blend or remineralisation.** A membrane rejects essentially everything, then you rebuild the water to target. Total control, handles chloride and silica, and it is the only answer for genuinely bad feed water. Costs: capital, space, a booster pump, waste water to drain, membrane replacement, and a more involved service regime.

**Acid dosing.** A metered food-grade acid injection converts bicarbonate to CO₂ directly. Precise and effective, and common in industrial water treatment. Rare in cafés because it needs dosing equipment, a chemical consumable, and monitoring — and a dosing failure has consequences in both directions.

**Nothing.** Genuinely the right answer when the feed water is already close to target. Carbon for chlorine, sediment protection, and leave the chemistry alone. Treating water that does not need treating is a real error, and it costs money forever.

## Compared

| | WAC cartridge | RO + blend/remin | Acid dosing | Untreated |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reduces alkalinity | Yes | Yes | Yes | No |
| Reduces hardness | Yes | Yes | No | No |
| Reduces chloride | No | Yes | No | No |
| Reduces silica | No | Yes | No | No |
| Capital cost | Low | High | Moderate | None |
| Running cost | Cartridges | Membranes, cartridges, water, power | Chemical | None |
| Waste water | None | Substantial | None | None |
| Space required | Small | Significant | Moderate | None |
| Power required | No | Usually | Yes | No |
| Service complexity | Low | Moderate–high | Moderate | Minimal |
| Fails by | Gradual leak-through | Membrane decline, blend drift | Dosing error | n/a |

The row that decides most jobs is **chloride**. It is the only parameter in the table that eliminates three of the four options outright.

## The decision tree

Work in this order, because each step can force the answer.

**1. Is chloride above ~30 mg/L, or silica above ~20–30 mg/L?**
Then it is RO. Nothing else touches either parameter, and no cartridge — however good — will change that. If chloride is high, use **remineralisation rather than blending**, because blending untreated feed back brings the chloride with it proportionally.

**2. Is alkalinity already in the 30–50 mg/L range?**
Then do not dealkalise. Fit carbon for chlorine and sediment protection, verify by titration, and stop. Document why you did not treat, so the next person does not assume it was an oversight.

**3. Is alkalinity moderate — roughly 50–200 mg/L?**
A WAC cartridge with a set bypass. This is the common case and the standard answer for a specialty bar.

**4. Is alkalinity very high — above roughly 250–300 mg/L?**
WAC still works chemically, but capacity collapses. Do the arithmetic: cartridge capacity at that hardness, against annual volume. If it means changing cartridges monthly, RO is cheaper within a couple of years and far less likely to be neglected.

**5. Is the feed already softened, with near-zero hardness and high sodium?**
WAC has little to exchange against, so alkalinity removal is poor. Either re-plumb upstream of the softener, or go to RO with remineralisation.

## Doing the arithmetic properly

Step four is where clients want a number rather than a judgement. The comparison worth putting in writing:

- **WAC annual cost** = (annual litres ÷ cartridge capacity *at measured hardness*) × cartridge price, plus labour per change.
- **RO annual cost** = membrane amortisation + prefilter and postfilter cartridges + water cost including reject + power, plus a heavier service visit.

Then note the things that are not in either figure: RO needs space and a drain, and it adds equipment that can fail. WAC needs somebody to actually change cartridges on schedule, which on a neglected site is a genuine risk.

I have specified WAC on sites where RO was marginally cheaper on paper, because the client had no space and a reliable service contract. I have specified RO on sites with moderate alkalinity because the chloride left no choice. The arithmetic informs the decision; it rarely makes it alone.

> Whichever you choose, the deliverable is not the equipment. It is **water measured at the machine inlet, landing in the target range, recorded and dated.** A perfectly specified system with an unverified blend setting is a guess with an invoice attached.

## What all four have in common

Carbon ahead of everything except sediment. Verification by titration at the machine inlet after full flushing. A recorded baseline. A change interval driven by measured volume. And a tag at the filter head so the next person knows what is fitted and when it is due.

Those five things matter more than which of the four columns you picked.
