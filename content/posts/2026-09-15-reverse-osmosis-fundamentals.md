Reverse osmosis is the answer when the feed water has something in it that ion exchange cannot address — chloride, silica, or simply far too much of everything. It buys total control over the final water at the cost of capital, space, waste and a real service regime.

## The principle

A semi-permeable membrane passes water molecules and rejects dissolved ions. Feed water is pushed against it under pressure; what passes through is **permeate**, and what is left behind, now more concentrated, is **reject** or **concentrate**, which goes to drain.

Rejection rates for a good membrane on a commercial system are typically 95–99% of dissolved solids. So permeate from a 300 mg/L feed might be 5–15 mg/L — near-pure water, and that is the problem as much as the point.

## Recovery, and the water you throw away

**Recovery** is permeate divided by feed. Small commercial systems commonly run 15–50%.

At 25% recovery, producing 1 litre of permeate sends roughly 3 litres to drain. That is a genuine cost and a genuine sustainability issue, and it is the fact clients are least prepared for. On a bar using 100 litres of treated water a day, an RO plant may be putting 300 litres down the drain.

Higher recovery reduces waste but concentrates the reject stream further, which increases scaling and fouling risk on the membrane itself. There is a real trade, and it is set at design time.

Two things worth telling a client before they sign: the water bill will rise, and the drain needs to accept a continuous trickle.

## Chlorine destroys the membrane

The most important operational fact about RO.

Thin-film composite membranes — the standard type — are **oxidised and destroyed by chlorine**. Not degraded slowly: destroyed, permanently, and the damage is not recoverable.

So the carbon prefilter ahead of an RO membrane is not there for taste. It is protecting the most expensive component in the system, and if it is neglected or undersized for peak flow, chlorine breakthrough will take the membrane out.

This makes carbon cartridge changes on an RO system considerably more consequential than on a cartridge system. It belongs in the schedule with an owner's name against it.

## The full train

A commercial RO installation is a sequence, not a single device:

1. **Sediment prefiltration** — often 5 micron then 1 micron absolute, protecting the membrane from particulate fouling.
2. **Carbon** — chlorine removal, sized on peak flow. Membrane protection.
3. **Booster pump**, where line pressure is insufficient. Membranes need pressure to work; inadequate pressure means poor rejection and poor output.
4. **The membrane**, in its housing, with the reject line to drain.
5. **Permeate storage**, usually a tank with a bladder, plus a delivery pump.
6. **Blending or remineralisation** — mandatory, covered in the next two posts.
7. **Post-carbon or polishing filter**, in some designs.

Each of those is a component that can fail, which is the honest cost of the control RO gives you.

## Temperature affects output

Membrane flux falls with temperature. Cold water is more viscous and passes more slowly, so **the same plant produces measurably less permeate in winter** than in summer.

This catches bars whose plant was sized in warm weather with little margin. The symptom is a permeate tank that cannot keep up with the morning rush in January, and a bar that runs out of treated water mid-service.

Size with margin for the coldest feed temperature, not the commissioning-day temperature.

## Fouling, scaling and membrane life

Membranes fail by three routes:

- **Particulate fouling** — inadequate prefiltration.
- **Scaling** on the concentrate side, where dissolved solids are concentrated. High recovery makes this worse; antiscalant dosing is used on larger systems.
- **Chemical attack** — chlorine, as above.

Expect two to three years on a well-prefiltered commercial membrane, less on difficult feed water. It is a consumable with a replacement cost, and it belongs in the client's lifecycle budget rather than arriving as a surprise.

## Monitoring — and the one place a TDS pen is exactly right

Membrane performance is monitored by **rejection rate**: measure conductivity in the feed and in the permeate, and compare.

This is the legitimate use of the instrument I have spent the month cautioning about. You are not characterising water chemistry; you are comparing two readings on the same system to detect a change. That is what a conductivity meter is genuinely good at.

Record feed and permeate readings at commissioning, then at every service. A rising permeate reading against a stable feed means the membrane is declining, and you can plan a replacement rather than react to one.

Also monitor: feed pressure, permeate flow, reject flow, and prefilter differential pressure. Five numbers, at every visit, in the log.

## Tank hygiene and stagnation

Permeate has no disinfectant residual and no buffering. A storage tank holding it is a place where bacteria can establish, particularly over a closure.

Sanitise the tank on a schedule, flush after extended shutdowns, and do not oversize the storage — a tank that turns over daily is much healthier than one holding a week's supply.

## Never plumb to unblended permeate

Stated once more because it is the failure that follows a well-installed RO plant.

Permeate is strongly undersaturated. It will dissolve carbonate from any protective layer it meets, then attack copper and brass. It has no buffering, so its pH wanders. It produces hollow, lifeless espresso. And its low conductivity can interfere with conductivity-based boiler level probes.

RO is not a complete treatment. It is the first half of one. The second half is the next two posts.
