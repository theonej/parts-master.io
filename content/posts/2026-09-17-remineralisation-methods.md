When RO was specified because of chloride or silica, blending untreated feed back is self-defeating. The alternative is to add specific minerals to the permeate — building the water you want rather than diluting the water you had.

This is more control and more equipment. It is also the only correct answer on genuinely bad feed water.

## What you are aiming to add

Back to the same targets:

- **Alkalinity 30–50 mg/L as CaCO₃** — for buffering and to stop the permeate being aggressive.
- **Calcium hardness 50–75 mg/L as CaCO₃** — for body and mouthfeel.
- **Some magnesium**, if you are choosing the composition, since it extracts slightly differently from calcium.
- **No chloride, no silica, no sodium** — the whole point.

## Calcite and magnesia cartridges

The simple approach: a cartridge of calcium carbonate media, sometimes with magnesium oxide blended in. Permeate passes through and dissolves some of it.

Because the permeate is undersaturated and slightly acidic, it dissolves calcite readily. This raises calcium hardness and alkalinity **together**, in the fixed ratio the chemistry dictates, and raises pH.

**Advantages.** No power, no chemical consumable, no dosing pump, small footprint. It fits the same cartridge housing culture as the rest of the train.

**The real limitation: output depends on contact time.** Dissolution is a rate process, so:

- At high flow, less contact, less mineral added.
- At low flow, more contact, more mineral added.

So the output concentration **varies with draw rate**. During a rush the water is less mineralised than during a quiet afternoon, and that is not something you can adjust away.

**And it depletes.** The media dissolves — that is the mechanism — so the bed shrinks and channels, and output falls over the cartridge's life. Output drifts downward continuously between changes.

**And the ratio is fixed.** Calcite gives you calcium and alkalinity in a set relationship. If your targets need a different ratio, a calcite cartridge cannot deliver it.

For most cafés this is an acceptable set of compromises, provided you accept that the output is a range rather than a number and you titrate at commissioning and every service.

## Proportional dosing pumps

A metering pump injects a prepared mineral concentrate into the permeate stream, proportional to flow.

**Advantages.** Precise, flow-compensated so it holds concentration across draw rates, and fully controllable — you choose the concentrate composition, so you set the calcium-to-magnesium-to-bicarbonate ratio deliberately rather than accepting what calcite gives.

**Costs.** A pump to power, maintain and eventually replace. A chemical consumable to stock and refill. A dosing failure that can go in either direction — no dosing gives aggressive permeate, over-dosing gives scale-forming water. It needs monitoring rather than trust.

This is the right answer on high-volume sites, on multi-machine installations, and anywhere the cup quality is a headline claim. It is over-specified for a two-group bar with a service contract.

## Mineral concentrates and packets

Prepared mineral formulations dosed by hand into a batch of water. Genuinely useful for competition, cupping and low-volume filter brewing, where precision matters and volume is small.

Not practical at bar volume, and not usable for a plumbed espresso machine. Worth knowing about because clients will have read about them and may ask.

## Choosing

| | Calcite cartridge | Dosing pump |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Precision | Moderate, flow-dependent | High, flow-compensated |
| Composition control | Fixed ratio | Fully chosen |
| Power required | No | Yes |
| Consumable | Media cartridge | Mineral concentrate |
| Drift | Output falls as media depletes | Stable if maintained |
| Failure mode | Gradual under-mineralisation | Either direction, faster |
| Suits | Most cafés | High volume, multi-site, quality-critical |

## Verification is not optional here

With blending, the output is a predictable fraction of a measured feed. With remineralisation it is the product of a rate process, so **calculation cannot substitute for measurement.**

At commissioning:

1. Flush the cartridge or prime the dosing system fully.
2. Titrate the output at the machine inlet — alkalinity and hardness.
3. Titrate again **at a realistic peak draw rate**, not just a trickle. On a calcite cartridge these two readings will differ, and you need to know by how much.
4. Adjust — dosing rate, or cartridge sizing, or add a second cartridge in parallel to increase contact time at high flow.
5. Record both readings, the flow rates they were taken at, and the equipment configuration.

That "two readings at two flow rates" step is the one that distinguishes a commissioned system from an installed one. A calcite cartridge that hits target at 1 L/min and delivers half that at 5 L/min is under-mineralising exactly when the bar is busiest — and the machine is seeing aggressive water during every rush.

> Remineralised water must be titrated at peak draw, not at a trickle. The whole weakness of dissolution-based media is flow dependence, and a single low-flow sample hides it completely.

## In service

- Titrate output at every visit, at a realistic flow.
- Check media level or concentrate level.
- Watch for a downward trend in alkalinity — media depletion.
- Change calcite media on schedule, not on appearance.
- On dosing systems, verify the pump is actually stroking and the concentrate is being drawn.

The failure to watch for is silent under-mineralisation: media depleted, output drifting toward permeate, and the machine's brass fittings quietly being attacked by water that was correct a year ago.
