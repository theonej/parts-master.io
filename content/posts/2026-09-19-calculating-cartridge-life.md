"Change the filter every six months" is the most common water instruction in the trade and it is arithmetic-free. The same cartridge on two sites can have change intervals a factor of four apart.

## Rated capacity is a function of your water

Ion exchange capacity is fixed in exchange sites, not in litres. How many litres those sites treat depends on how much hardness and alkalinity each litre contains.

Manufacturers publish tables — capacity against feed hardness, usually in German degrees. The shape is roughly inverse:

| Feed hardness | Indicative capacity |
| --- | --- |
| 5 °dH (~89 mg/L) | 12,000 L |
| 10 °dH (~179 mg/L) | 6,500 L |
| 15 °dH (~268 mg/L) | 4,200 L |
| 20 °dH (~357 mg/L) | 3,200 L |

Illustrative figures — use the manufacturer's own table for the cartridge you are fitting. The point is the dependency: **you cannot know the interval without the water test.**

Bypass changes it too. More bypass means less water through the resin, so longer life — which is one of its benefits.

## Real consumption is much higher than the drinks

The other half of the calculation, and where estimates go badly wrong.

A double espresso is perhaps 40 ml in the cup. The water the machine actually consumed to produce it is several times that, because you also spent:

- Group flush before dosing.
- Backflushing at close, spread across the day's drinks.
- Steam boiler refill — every steamed jug is water that left as steam.
- Purging the steam wand, twice per milk drink.
- Cooling flushes, on HX machines.
- Hot water for Americanos and tea, which is pure volume.

A workable planning figure is **0.2–0.3 litres of treated water per espresso-based drink**, and considerably more if the bar sells a lot of Americanos, tea or batch filter. Those go in as their actual volumes on top.

Using 40 ml per drink — which people do — underestimates cartridge consumption by a factor of five or more. That is how a bar ends up running four months past due while believing it is early.

## The calculation

Worked, for a bar selling 300 espresso-based drinks and 40 Americanos a day, six days a week, on 10 °dH water:

- Espresso drinks: 300 × 0.25 L = **75 L/day**
- Americanos: 40 × 0.2 L = **8 L/day**
- Daily total: **83 L**
- Weekly: 83 × 6 = **498 L**
- Cartridge capacity at 10 °dH: **6,500 L**
- **6,500 ÷ 498 ≈ 13 weeks**

So roughly a quarterly change — not six months. Then apply margin.

## Change at 80–85% of rated capacity

Because exhaustion is not a cliff.

Weak acid cation resin **leaks progressively**. As the bed approaches exhaustion, treated alkalinity climbs steadily. The last 15–20% of rated capacity is already delivering degraded water, and the machine is scaling faster than the baseline throughout that period.

So on the example above: 6,500 × 0.82 ≈ 5,300 L, which at 498 L/week is about **11 weeks**. Set the interval there.

> Rated capacity is where the cartridge stops working. The change interval should be where it stops working *well*, which is meaningfully earlier and completely invisible without a volume measurement.

## Getting the volume figure

In descending order of reliability:

**A water meter.** The right answer, cheap, and it removes all estimation. Tomorrow's post covers it.

**The machine's shot counter,** where fitted. Gives espresso drinks directly; add hot water and steam separately.

**POS data.** Drinks by category, which is what the calculation above uses. Good, provided someone applies realistic per-drink volumes.

**A guess.** What most bars do. Systematically optimistic.

## The calendar backstop

Even at very low volume, change annually. Media degrades, carbon beds support bacterial growth in stagnant water, and a cartridge sitting for three years at 30% of rated volume is not a cartridge in good condition.

This matters for low-volume sites, second machines, and any bar that went quiet for a season.

## What to record

On the tag at the filter head and in the water log:

- Cartridge model and rated capacity **at the measured feed hardness**, not the headline figure.
- Install date.
- **Meter reading at install.**
- Bypass setting.
- Calculated due volume — meter reading at install plus 82% of rated capacity.
- Post-flush titration result.

The due *volume* is the useful figure, not a due date. Then a monthly meter read tells anyone whether it is due, without arithmetic or memory.

## Verify by titration anyway

The calculation schedules the change. Titration confirms the cartridge is still doing its job.

At every service, titrate the treated output at the machine inlet. Alkalinity creeping up against the commissioning baseline means one of three things: the cartridge is nearing exhaustion, the bypass has drifted, or the feed water has changed seasonally.

All three are worth knowing, and all three are invisible if the only check is a date on a label.
