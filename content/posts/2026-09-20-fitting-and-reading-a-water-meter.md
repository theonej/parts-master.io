Everything in yesterday's calculation depends on knowing how much water has passed through the cartridge. A meter turns that from an estimate into a reading, and it costs less than a single cartridge.

I fit one on every installation now, and the only regret is on jobs where I did not.

## What it buys

**Cartridge scheduling that is actually correct.** Due volume minus current reading. No estimation, no arguing about whether a quiet January counts.

**Leak detection, free.** Read the meter at close and again at open with everything switched off. The numbers should be identical. If the meter has moved overnight, water is going somewhere — a weeping fitting, a passing solenoid, a failing expansion valve, or a drain trickle.

This is a genuinely valuable diagnostic and it takes ten seconds. A slow leak inside a counter void can run for months before anyone notices the damage, and the overnight meter comparison finds it the first time anyone bothers to look.

**Consumption trends.** Rising consumption per drink over months usually means something is being wasted — a longer flush habit, a passing valve, an autofill cycling more than it should.

**Evidence.** A warranty conversation about scale is much stronger with meter readings alongside dated titration results, because together they demonstrate the cartridge was changed within its rated volume on the measured water.

## Choosing one

- **Potable-approved**, appropriate for drinking water contact.
- **Sized for the line**, typically 15 mm or 20 mm on a café supply. An oversized meter under-reads at low flow, which matters because much of a bar's consumption happens as small draws.
- **Totalising**, reading in litres. A cumulative total is what you need; instantaneous flow rate is a bonus.
- **Mechanical is fine.** Simple, no battery, decades of life. Electronic versions add alarms and remote reading, which suit multi-site operations.
- **Rated for the pressure and temperature** of a cold feed line.

## Where to fit it

The position determines what the number means, so decide deliberately.

**What you want to measure is the volume passing through the cartridge**, because that is what determines cartridge life.

- **With no bypass**, anywhere in series with the filter works — before or after gives the same total.
- **With a bypass**, the distinction matters. A meter on the inlet to the whole assembly counts water that partly went around the media. A meter positioned to count only what passes the resin gives you true media loading, but a meter on the total feed is easier to fit and is what manufacturers' capacity tables generally assume anyway.

In practice: **fit it on the feed to the filter assembly**, downstream of the isolation valve, and use the manufacturer's capacity figure on that basis. Simple, consistent, and it also gives you total site consumption for the leak test.

Practical install notes:

- **Observe the flow direction arrow.** A meter fitted backwards does not count.
- **Allow the straight lengths** upstream and downstream that the manufacturer specifies — turbulence from an adjacent elbow affects accuracy.
- **Mount so the dial is readable** without a torch and a mirror. This sounds trivial and determines whether anyone ever reads it.
- **Isolation valve upstream**, so the meter can be replaced.
- **Support it.** Do not hang it off the pipework.

## Reading it, as a routine

Add one line to the monthly maintenance sheet:

> **Water meter reading: _______ litres. Cartridge due at: _______ litres.**

That is the whole system. Anyone can complete it, no arithmetic is needed to see whether a change is approaching, and the trend is visible across the sheet.

Then add the leak check to the close-down routine weekly rather than daily — read at close, read at open, flag any difference.

## Built-in indicators

Many filter heads have their own volume indicator or a coloured dial that advances with use. These are useful and worth using.

Two cautions. They are often approximate, and they are calibrated on assumptions about your bypass setting and feed water. And they cannot do the overnight leak test, because they only count what passes the filter and are usually not readable to the litre.

An indicator on the head plus an actual meter is the belt-and-braces arrangement, and the meter is the one I would keep if forced to choose.

## On existing sites

Retrofitting is a twenty-minute plumbing job on an accessible feed, and it is one of the highest-value small upgrades available on a neglected bar.

If a client has no idea when their cartridge was last changed and no records, fit a meter, change the cartridge, record the reading, and you have converted an unknown into a managed schedule from that day forward. That single intervention often does more for the machine's remaining life than anything else on the visit.

> A meter is the cheapest instrument on the bar and the only one that makes cartridge scheduling honest. Fit one on every installation, and retrofit one on every site you inherit.
