Most commercial filter heads have a bypass setting — a numbered dial or a selectable port. It is set at installation, usually to whatever it arrived on, and rarely thought about again.

It is one of only two adjustments you get on a cartridge system, and it directly determines the water the machine drinks.

## What it does mechanically

The bypass routes a controlled proportion of the incoming water **around** the treatment media and remixes it with the treated stream at the outlet.

So the output is a blend: mostly treated water, plus a deliberate fraction of untreated feed.

## Why you want some

Full treatment through the resin is not the goal. Three reasons:

**Hardness. ** Fully treated output has very little calcium, which gives thin, hollow espresso. The bypass brings hardness back toward the 50–75 mg/L target.

**Alkalinity should not be zero.** A little buffering is protective. Water with no alkalinity has no resistance to pH movement and is mildly aggressive to brass and copper — the saturation argument from earlier in the month. Keeping 30–50 mg/L is deliberate.

**Cartridge life.** Water that goes around the media does not consume exchange capacity. More bypass means fewer litres through the resin and a longer interval.

So bypass is not a compromise or a cost-saving. It is how you land on target instead of overshooting it.

## The trade-off

| More bypass | Less bypass |
| --- | --- |
| Higher output hardness and alkalinity | Lower output hardness and alkalinity |
| Longer cartridge life | Shorter cartridge life |
| More chloride and silica passed through | Less chloride and silica passed through |

That third row is the one people miss, and it is the same caveat as RO blending: **bypassed water carries everything in the feed, proportionally.** On a high-chloride supply, opening the bypass to hit a hardness target also raises chloride toward the pitting threshold.

If chloride is your problem, a cartridge with bypass is not the right architecture at all.

## The factory default is a guess about your water

Bypass dial positions correspond to nominal blend fractions, and manufacturers set a sensible default for typical feed water.

Your water is not typical water. A default position calibrated for moderate hardness delivers over-mineralised output on very hard feed and stripped output on soft feed. Leaving it where it arrived is leaving the specification to a factory that has never tested your supply.

I have found factory defaults still in place on machines that were scaling steadily, on water twice as hard as the setting assumed.

## Setting it

1. **Measure the feed** — alkalinity and calcium hardness by titration, plus chloride from the lab analysis.
2. **Calculate roughly.** Output ≈ feed × bypass fraction, since treated output is low. To get 40 mg/L alkalinity from 200 mg/L feed, you want about 20%.
3. **Check both targets** against that fraction. If hardness and alkalinity will not both land, prioritise alkalinity.
4. **Run the chloride number through it.** Feed chloride × fraction must stay under 30 mg/L.
5. **Set the dial** to the nearest position.
6. **Flush the cartridge** fully first — a pre-flush reading is meaningless.
7. **Titrate the output at the machine inlet.** Downstream of every tee, at the point the machine draws from.
8. **Adjust and re-titrate** until it lands.
9. **Record the position** and the readings that produced it, and mark the dial physically.

> The dial position is a starting point. The titration is the answer. A bypass set from the label without verification is a specification nobody has checked.

## Why it drifts off target

The same four mechanisms as an RO blend:

- **Feed water changes seasonally**, so the same fraction gives different output.
- **The cartridge ages**, and treated output alkalinity rises as the resin leaks — which the bypass then adds to.
- **The dial gets moved** by someone working in the cabinet.
- **Pressure balance shifts** as the sediment stage loads, which in some arrangements changes the ratio.

None of these announce themselves. A machine can scale for a year on a system that was correct at commissioning.

## In the service routine

At every visit: titrate the output at the machine inlet, compare against the commissioning baseline, confirm the dial is still on its mark, and re-set if the feed has moved.

Then record it. The value is in the series — an output alkalinity figure that has climbed from 40 to 70 over three visits tells you something specific, and a single reading tells you almost nothing.
