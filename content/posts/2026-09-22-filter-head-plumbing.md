Every water treatment decision this month is undone by one thing: a filter head mounted where nobody can service it.

A cartridge that takes forty minutes and a dismantled cabinet to change gets postponed. Postponed becomes skipped. And a cartridge past exhaustion is not neutral — it is passing untreated water while the client believes they are protected, which is worse than having no filter at all, because nobody is worried.

## Clearance is the whole thing

Measure the cartridge's **removal envelope**, not its size. Depending on the head design, a cartridge either drops straight down, swings on a bayonet, or twists and pulls.

- **Drop-down heads** need the cartridge's full length below the head, plus a hand's width.
- **Bayonet and twist heads** need swing clearance to the side.
- Either way, **check the manufacturer's stated service clearance** and treat it as a minimum.

The classic failure is a head mounted with 50 mm beneath it in a neat cabinet. The cartridge cannot come out without unbolting the head from the wall and disconnecting the plumbing — turning a five-minute consumable change into a plumbing job.

> Change the cartridge once, for real, with everything installed, before you leave the commissioning visit. If it is awkward for you with the right tools, it will not happen at all once you are gone.

## Mounting

- **Bracket to a solid surface.** Never hang a head off its own pipework — the cartridge is heavy when full and the change action applies real torque.
- **Upright, per the manufacturer.** Orientation affects air trapping and, on some designs, the internal flow path.
- **Off the floor.** Floors in a coffee bar get wet, and a head at floor level sits in whatever leaks.
- **Out of the direct wet zone**, away from where a drain box can overflow onto it.
- **Not against the espresso machine's hot side.** Warm cartridges age faster and support bacterial growth.

## The plumbing around it

**An isolation valve upstream**, reachable and capable of actually shutting off. Test it at commissioning — a valve that has never been closed sometimes will not.

**A flush point.** This is the fitting most often omitted and most needed: a tee with a valve downstream of the head, with a hose to a bucket or drain, so a new cartridge can be flushed **without pushing carbon fines through the machine.** Without it, people flush through the group, which is exactly how fines reach gicleurs and flowmeters.

**Pressure gauges**, before the assembly and after it. The differential is your cartridge-loading indicator and your peak-flow diagnostic. Two cheap gauges replace a great deal of guesswork.

**A water meter** on the feed, per yesterday's post.

**Unions either side**, so the head can be removed without cutting pipe.

**Space for a second cartridge.** If there is any chance the bar grows a batch brewer or a hot water tap, leave room for a parallel manifold. Retrofitting one into a full cabinet is far more disruptive than allowing 200 mm now.

## Labelling

A tag at the head, filled in at every change:

- Cartridge model and rated capacity at the measured feed hardness
- Install date
- Meter reading at install
- Due volume
- Bypass setting
- Post-flush titration result: alkalinity and hardness

That tag is the minimum viable water log, and it survives staff turnover in a way that a folder does not. Anyone opening the cabinet can see what is fitted and whether it is due.

Also label the isolation valve and the flush valve. An unlabelled valve in a cabinet gets closed by someone guessing.

## Light and access

Two mundane requirements that determine whether maintenance happens.

**An access door big enough for shoulders**, positioned in front of the equipment rather than around a corner from it. A void reached by lying on the floor and stretching sideways does not get inspected.

**Light.** Nobody titrates a sample, reads a gauge or spots a weep in the dark. A cheap battery light stuck inside the cabinet is a real improvement.

## Commissioning checklist

1. Change the cartridge in place, fully, without moving anything else.
2. Close and reopen the isolation valve; confirm it shuts.
3. Flush a cartridge through the flush point to a bucket; confirm it works and does not flood the void.
4. Read both gauges; record clean differential.
5. Read and record the meter.
6. Titrate the output at the machine inlet.
7. Fill in the tag.
8. Photograph the void with everything labelled, for the handover file.
