Every cartridge datasheet specifies a flushing volume. It is one of the most frequently ignored instructions in the trade, partly because the volumes look excessive and partly because nothing obviously bad happens when you skip it.

Something bad does happen. It just happens somewhere else, later.

## What you are flushing out

**Carbon fines.** New carbon sheds fine black dust — from manufacture, handling and transport. Enough to visibly darken the first litres.

**Resin fines and preservative.** Ion exchange media carries manufacturing residue, and some cartridges are supplied with a preservative to prevent bacterial growth in storage.

**Trapped air.** A dry cartridge is full of air, which has to be displaced. Air pockets cause erratic flow, noise, and readings that make no sense.

**Whatever settled during storage.** Media beds settle and channel in a box on a shelf. Initial flow re-establishes an even bed.

## Never flush through the machine

The single most important point.

If the first water through a new cartridge goes into the espresso machine, the fines go with it. They arrive at:

- **Gicleurs** — 0.5–0.8 mm orifices, which is exactly the scale at which fines cause partial blockage. This produces one slow group, on one machine, for reasons nobody connects to a filter change three weeks earlier.
- **Solenoid valve seats**, where a particle holds a valve slightly open.
- **Flowmeters**, where fines foul the impeller and volumetric doses drift short.
- **The pump**, and downstream into the boiler.

All of those are diagnosed later as separate faults. They were one avoidable shortcut.

This is why the flush point matters — a tee and a valve downstream of the filter head, with a hose to a bucket or a drain. Without it there is no way to flush except through the machine, so the installation guarantees the mistake.

> Flush to a bucket, never through the group. A cartridge change that puts carbon fines through a flowmeter costs more to diagnose than the cartridge cost to buy.

## How much

Whatever the manufacturer states. Volumes vary widely by product — some want a few litres, some want considerably more, and combination cartridges with carbon and resin generally want more than a plain sediment stage.

Two rules, and apply both:

1. **The stated volume**, measured — count buckets, or use the water meter.
2. **Until it runs visibly clear**, whichever is greater. If it is still tinted at the stated volume, keep going.

Using the meter for this is neat: note the reading, flush, and stop at the target. It also means the flushing volume is captured in the log.

## Purge the air properly

Flush until flow is **steady and continuous**, with no spitting or surging. Air locks in a cartridge housing can persist through a short flush and then release later, producing a slug of air into the machine.

On multi-stage trains, work through in sequence and confirm steady flow at each stage.

## Then test — not before

A sample taken before flushing is meaningless. It reflects preservative, fines and whatever was sitting in the media, not the cartridge's steady-state performance.

So the order is: flush fully, then titrate the output at the machine inlet. Alkalinity and calcium hardness, plus total chlorine.

This matters for bypass setting too. Setting a bypass from a pre-flush reading means setting it from noise.

## Flush after idle periods too

Not only on installation.

Carbon is a nutrient-rich surface in dechlorinated water. Over a two-week holiday closure the bed stagnates, warms toward room temperature and grows bacteria. The same applies to an RO permeate tank, which has no disinfectant residual at all.

**Flush the treatment train before service resumes after any extended closure.** Several litres to drain. It takes two minutes and it is the difference between the first coffee back tasting normal and tasting of the cupboard.

Add it to the reopening checklist alongside the machine warm-up.

## Record it

In the water log and on the tag:

- Date of change
- Cartridge model and rated capacity at measured feed hardness
- Meter reading at install
- **Volume flushed**
- Post-flush titration: alkalinity, hardness, total chlorine
- Bypass setting confirmed

The flush volume is worth recording because it is the line that tells the next person the job was done properly. It is also the line that, when blank, explains a blocked gicleur three weeks later.

## The instruction for bar staff

If the client changes their own cartridges — which is reasonable for a simple system — the written instruction needs a number in it, like every other routine:

> **Close the isolation valve. Change the cartridge. Open the valve. Open the flush valve and run 10 litres into the bucket. Close the flush valve. Write the date and the meter reading on the tag.**

Numbers survive. "Flush it through" does not.
