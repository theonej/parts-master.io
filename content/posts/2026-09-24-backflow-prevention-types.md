An espresso machine is connected to the potable supply and contains heated water in a pressurised vessel, with chemicals used on it periodically. From a water authority's point of view that is a contamination risk pointing back at the mains.

Backflow prevention is therefore a code requirement rather than good practice, and it is one inspectors know exactly where to look for.

I am going to be careful here: requirements are jurisdictional and hazard categories are defined locally. What follows is the shape of the problem, not a substitute for your local water regulations.

## The two mechanisms

**Back-siphonage.** Supply pressure drops — a mains burst, a fire draw, upstream work — creating negative pressure that siphons water backwards out of connected equipment.

**Back-pressure.** The equipment's own pressure exceeds supply pressure and pushes water back up the feed. An espresso machine with a heated, pressurised boiler is capable of exactly this, which is why it is treated as a back-pressure hazard rather than only a siphonage one.

## Device categories

Generic types, in ascending order of protection:

**Single and double check valves.** One or two spring-loaded non-return valves. Simple, cheap, no maintenance access. Suitable for lower hazard categories. A double check valve is the common minimum on a machine feed.

**Air gap / atmospheric vacuum breaker.** A physical break to atmosphere. Very effective against siphonage, useless against back-pressure, and it must be installed above the equipment with no downstream valve.

**Pressure-type vacuum breaker.** Works under continuous pressure, unlike an atmospheric one.

**Reduced pressure zone device (RPZ).** Two check valves with a relief valve between them, maintaining a zone at reduced pressure. Protects against both mechanisms, used for higher hazard categories. **Testable, and typically requires periodic testing and certification by an approved tester** — an ongoing obligation the client needs to know about.

**Break tank / type AA or AB air gap.** Complete physical separation, feeding a tank open to atmosphere. Highest protection. Used where the hazard category demands it, and on some water-fed equipment as standard.

## Which one you need

Determined by the local hazard categorisation for commercial catering and coffee equipment, and it genuinely varies. In some jurisdictions a double check valve on the machine feed satisfies the requirement; in others the installation attracts a higher category and needs an RPZ or a break tank.

The practical route:

1. **Ask the local water authority** or read the applicable water supply regulations for your area.
2. **Check the machine manual** — some manufacturers specify a device.
3. **Get it confirmed in writing** where the answer is not obvious, and put that confirmation in the handover file.
4. **Have a competent plumber install it** to code, and get the certification.

Do not guess and do not copy what was on the last job in a different jurisdiction. This is one of the few areas in this month's writing where the answer is genuinely "look it up locally".

## The drain is a separate requirement

Backflow protection on the **supply** is distinct from indirect waste on the **drain**.

The machine's drain must terminate above a floor sink, gully or standpipe with a visible air gap, so a blocked sewer cannot push waste back into the drip tray. Directly connecting the drain into the waste stack is a separate code failure from missing supply protection, and it is the one inspectors find first.

Both are required. Neither substitutes for the other.

## The side effect nobody plans for

Here is the consequence that matters for the machine.

**A check valve or backflow preventer turns the machine's water circuit into a closed system.** Water can get in but not back out.

Water expands when heated — roughly 4% between cold and boiling. In a closed system that expansion has nowhere to go, so pressure rises steeply during every warm-up.

Symptoms:

- The brew-side expansion valve relieving to drain during warm-up, every day.
- Brew pressure spiking above setpoint.
- Weeping fittings and fatigued joints over time.
- Gauges reading high at heat-up and normal later.

This is not a fault with the backflow device. It is the predictable consequence of fitting one, and the fix is an expansion vessel — which is tomorrow's post.

> Every correctly installed backflow preventer creates a thermal expansion problem. Fit the device because code requires it, then fit an expansion vessel because the device requires it. Installing the first without the second is a very common and entirely avoidable source of leaks.

## For the handover file

- Device type fitted, position, and who installed it.
- The regulatory basis — which category, which regulation.
- Certification, where the device is testable.
- **Next test due date** for an RPZ, with the obligation explained to the client in writing.
- A photograph of the installed device and of the drain air gap.

That last item costs nothing and answers an inspector's question in seconds, months after everyone has forgotten the detail.
