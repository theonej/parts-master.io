The bang from inside the counter when the machine's autofill cycles is not a quirk. It is a pressure wave, and it is doing damage every time.

## The mechanism

Water in a pipe has mass and momentum. Close a valve quickly and that moving column has to stop almost instantly. Its kinetic energy converts into a pressure wave that travels back up the pipe at close to the speed of sound in water, reflects, and travels back.

The peak pressure can be many times static pressure. It is a genuine transient overpressure event, repeated every time the valve closes.

Three factors set how bad it is:

- **Flow velocity.** The dominant term. Faster water means more momentum to stop.
- **Closure speed.** Solenoids close very fast, which is exactly the problem.
- **Pipe run length.** Longer runs contain more moving mass.

## Sources on a coffee bar

- **Autofill solenoids**, cycling many times an hour. The most common source, and the one that correlates with the noise.
- **The machine's inlet solenoid.**
- **Quick-closing taps and lever valves.**
- **Pump check valves.**
- **Dishwashers and ice machines** on the same branch, contributing their own transients.

The autofill case is the significant one because of frequency. A three-group machine refilling repeatedly through a busy morning delivers hundreds of pressure transients a day into the same joints.

## Why it matters beyond the noise

The bang is the audible symptom. The damage is cumulative:

- **Joints loosen** under repeated cycling, then weep.
- **Fittings fatigue** and eventually crack — often at a compression olive or a thread root.
- **Braided hoses degrade** faster than their rated life.
- **Gauges and pressure switches** get hammered and drift or fail.
- **Pipe clips and supports work loose**, which increases movement and makes the next transient worse.

This is a real cause of the "mystery leak" that appears eighteen months after a clean installation. The joint did not fail spontaneously; it was fatigued by a few hundred thousand pressure cycles.

> If you can hear water hammer, it is fatiguing the installation. Treat the noise as a fault report, not a characteristic of the building.

## Diagnosis

Straightforward, because it is audible and correlated.

1. **Listen while the machine cycles.** Trigger an autofill by drawing hot water, and listen for the bang as the fill valve closes.
2. **Correlate the timing** with valve operation. Hammer coincides with closure, not opening.
3. **Check static and dynamic pressure.** High pressure makes hammer worse and is often the underlying cause.
4. **Look at pipe support** along the run. Unsupported pipe moves visibly and audibly.
5. **Consider velocity.** Undersized pipe for the flow means high velocity.

## The cures, in order

**Reduce pressure.** Fit or correct a pressure regulator. This is the first move because it addresses the root cause, reduces velocity, and helps everything else on the installation. Also check for overnight pressure rise, which makes night-time transients worse than what you measure at midday.

**Reduce velocity.** Correctly sized pipe. Aim to keep velocity below roughly 1.5–2 m/s. An undersized supply run produces high velocity even at moderate pressure, and no arrestor fully compensates for it.

**Support the pipe properly.** Clip it at the manufacturer's recommended spacing along the whole run, with particular attention near valves and direction changes. Unsupported pipe converts a pressure transient into physical movement, which is both the noise and the fatigue. This is often the cheapest and most effective single intervention.

**Fit an arrestor near the source.** A water hammer arrestor — a small sealed cylinder with an air charge or a piston — absorbs the transient. It must be fitted **close to the valve causing the problem**, because its effectiveness falls off with distance. An arrestor at the far end of the run does very little.

**The expansion vessel helps.** If you have already fitted one for thermal expansion, it provides some absorption. It is not a substitute for a properly placed arrestor, but it contributes.

**Avoid old-style air chambers.** A capped vertical pipe stub relies on trapped air, and the air dissolves into the water over weeks. They work for a month and then stop, silently.

## Order of work on a noisy install

1. Measure static and dynamic pressure; fit or adjust a regulator.
2. Check and correct pipe support along the entire run.
3. Verify pipe sizing against peak flow.
4. Fit an arrestor adjacent to the offending solenoid if noise persists.
5. Re-check for weeping joints, because existing hammer may already have fatigued some.

That last step matters. Fixing the hammer does not repair the joints it has already loosened, so go round the fittings afterwards and check them — cold and hot, static and flowing.

## Prevention at installation

- Regulate pressure to mid-range of the machine's specification.
- Size the supply run for the peak simultaneous flow.
- Clip the pipework properly, first time.
- Keep the run as short and direct as the layout allows.
- Record static and dynamic pressure in the baseline.

None of it is expensive at installation. All of it is disruptive to retrofit inside a finished counter.
