The treatment was designed from a sample. Samples are a moment, and municipal water is not a constant.

This is the reason a service contract should include water testing, and the reason a bar can start scaling two years after a perfectly correct installation with nobody having touched anything.

## What actually changes

**Source blending.** Utilities draw from several sources and shift the mix according to demand, rainfall and maintenance. A supply that is 80% borehole in winter can be 50% surface water in summer, and those have quite different chemistry. Your zone's reported average smooths this out completely.

**Rainfall and dilution.** Heavy rain dilutes surface sources — lower hardness, lower alkalinity, often more organics and turbidity. Drought concentrates them.

**Temperature.** Warmer water in summer affects taste, chlorine demand and bacterial growth, and reduces RO membrane output when cold in winter.

**Algal activity** in surface reservoirs through summer, raising organics and creating taste and odour that carbon has to handle.

**Road salt.** The one to watch. Winter de-icing raises **chloride** in shallow groundwater and surface sources, sometimes considerably. A supply tested in July at 20 mg/L chloride can be materially higher in February, which moves it toward the pitting threshold at exactly the time nobody is testing.

**Disinfectant switching.** Some utilities move between free chlorine and chloramine seasonally. A carbon stage sized for free chlorine will pass chloramine, so the water tastes faintly medicinal for part of the year and clean for the rest.

**Mains work.** A repair in the street stirs up sediment and iron, and can change which source the zone is fed from.

## What that does downstream

- **Cartridge life changes.** Capacity is a function of feed hardness. Harder feed in late summer means the interval calculated in spring is now optimistic.
- **Bypass and blend settings drift off target.** The fraction was calculated from one feed measurement. Same valve position, different output.
- **Scale rate changes**, tracking alkalinity.
- **Taste changes**, and gets blamed on the roaster. Bars change coffee suppliers over seasonal water shifts.
- **Chloride risk appears** in winter and is gone by the time anyone samples.

> A blend or bypass set from a single feed measurement is correct for that week. Everything after that is drift, and none of it announces itself.

## The re-test regime

**Every six months, minimum.** Alkalinity, calcium hardness and total chlorine by field titration, at the machine inlet, plus the feed for comparison.

**Ideally quarterly**, aligned with the service visits already happening.

**Time one of them for late winter** if the site is anywhere affected by road salt or is coastal. That is when chloride peaks, and a summer-only testing regime will never see it.

**Additionally re-test after:**

- Mains work in the street, or any reported supply interruption.
- Any change to the building's internal plumbing.
- A softener being installed elsewhere in the building — which happens without anyone thinking to mention it.
- A cartridge change, as routine verification.
- Any complaint about taste that cannot be traced to coffee or process.

**Send a full lab analysis** again if anything looks structurally different — particularly if sodium has appeared or chloride has moved.

## What to do with the result

Not just record it. Act:

1. **Compare against the commissioning baseline** and the last reading.
2. **Re-calculate the bypass or blend fraction** from the new feed figures.
3. **Adjust and re-titrate** the output at the machine inlet until it lands in target.
4. **Re-calculate cartridge life** at the new feed hardness — the interval may have shortened.
5. **Record all of it** with the date.

Step four is the one that gets missed. A bar on a schedule set from spring water can be running cartridges 30% past their real capacity by autumn, with the output alkalinity climbing and nobody looking.

## The trend is the value

A single reading tells you today. The series tells you what is happening.

- **Feed alkalinity rising year on year** — a genuine supply change, and the specification may need revisiting.
- **Treated alkalinity rising while feed is stable** — cartridge exhausting early, or the bypass has moved.
- **Chloride appearing in winter readings only** — seasonal salt, and worth a conversation about RO if it is reaching the threshold.
- **Everything stable for three years** — genuinely useful information, and grounds for relaxing the interval slightly.

## Explaining it to the client

This is one of the easier arguments to make, because it is concrete.

Their water is not a fixed thing. The treatment was designed for what came out of the tap on one day. Checking it twice a year takes ten minutes and keeps the machine inside the manufacturer's warranty conditions — and the record of those checks is what answers a declined scale claim.

Framed that way, water testing stops looking like a line item on a service contract and starts looking like the cheapest insurance on the equipment.
