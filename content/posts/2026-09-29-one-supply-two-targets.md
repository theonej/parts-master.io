A bar serving espresso and batch filter has two applications with slightly different ideal water, fed from one supply. The question of whether to split treatment comes up on most specialty installations.

My answer for most bars is no, and it is worth explaining why rather than just asserting it.

## The two targets

**Espresso.** The constraint is the machine: a pressurised boiler above 120 °C, with small orifices in the brew path and an element whose life is set by deposit. That pushes alkalinity toward the low end — 30–50 mg/L as CaCO₃ — with calcium hardness 50–75.

**Filter.** The SCA brewing standard targets around 40 mg/L alkalinity and 68 mg/L calcium hardness. Notably, that is not far from the espresso figures at all.

Where filter differs is in **tolerance**. A batch brewer runs cooler, at atmospheric pressure, with no small orifices and a replaceable element in many cases. It is far more forgiving of higher alkalinity. And filter brewing arguably benefits from slightly more mineral content for body and sweetness, because there is no crema and no pressure to carry texture.

So the honest summary: the targets are close, filter is more forgiving, and filter can take a little more mineral if you want it.

## Why one train usually wins

If you treat the whole supply to the espresso target, the filter coffee is brewed on water slightly below its ideal mineral content. The difference in the cup is real and small.

Against that, a second treatment train costs you:

- **A second cartridge and head** to buy and house, in a cabinet that is already full.
- **A second change schedule**, with its own volume tracking and its own tag.
- **A second thing to forget.** This is the decisive point. Two systems means two cartridges to monitor, and the low-volume one is the one that gets neglected — the same pattern as the decaf grinder.
- **A second set of readings** at every service.
- **More plumbing**, more joints, more to leak.

A single train to the espresso target, properly maintained, beats two trains where one is six months overdue. And that is the realistic comparison, not the idealised one.

> Never run the espresso machine on the filter target. The boiler is the binding constraint, and it does not care that the brewer would have preferred more alkalinity.

## When splitting is genuinely worth it

Three cases where I would do it:

**A serious filter programme.** Where brewed coffee is a headline offer, single origins rotate, and someone is measuring extraction. Then the marginal cup improvement is part of the product, and there is usually also someone competent to maintain a second system.

**Very bad source water requiring RO.** Once an RO plant is installed, you already have permeate available. Producing two blends from one permeate stream is much cheaper than two independent treatment trains — a second blend or remineralisation leg, not a second membrane. This is the case where splitting is genuinely economical.

**High volume where cartridge life is the constraint.** Sometimes splitting is about capacity rather than chemistry: taking the hot water tap and brewer off a separate parallel manifold to keep flow and cartridge life sane on the espresso leg.

That third case is worth noting because it is really the peak-flow problem from earlier in the month wearing a different hat. Splitting for capacity is a sizing decision, not a taste one.

## If you do split

- **Label everything, clearly and physically.** Two identical cartridges in one cabinet feeding different equipment is a mistake waiting to happen.
- **Separate tags**, separate volumes, separate due dates.
- **Separate meters**, or at least separate volume tracking.
- **Both legs titrated** at every service, at their own outlets.
- **One log sheet covering both**, so a glance shows if one is lagging.

The discipline has to be deliberate, because the failure mode is asymmetric neglect.

## Hot water for tea is a third case

Worth flagging since it lives on the same supply.

Hot water drawn from the espresso machine for tea and Americanos comes from the steam boiler, which means it has been sitting at temperature, has lost dissolved gases, and on some machines carries a slightly stale character. That is a machine and workflow question rather than a treatment one, and it gets its own post in February.

For treatment purposes, hot water draw matters mainly because it is **volume** — it consumes cartridge capacity fast and it dominates peak flow calculations. A bar selling a lot of tea has a shorter cartridge interval than its espresso count suggests.

## The practical recommendation

For a typical two- or three-group specialty bar with a batch brewer: **one treatment train, specified to the espresso target, sized for peak simultaneous flow including the hot water tap and the brewer, on a parallel manifold if the flow calculation demands it.**

Then put the effort into verification and scheduling rather than into a second system. A single train that is titrated twice a year and changed on measured volume will do more for both the espresso and the filter than two trains that nobody is tracking.
