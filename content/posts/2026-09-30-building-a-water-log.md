A month of water chemistry reduces to one operational practice: write the numbers down, dated, and keep them somewhere they will survive.

Everything else in September is a technique. This is the thing that makes the techniques compound.

## Why the series matters more than the reading

A single measurement tells you the state today. It cannot tell you whether that state is normal, improving or deteriorating — and almost every useful water question is about change.

- Treated alkalinity of 55 mg/L. Fine, or a cartridge two-thirds exhausted? Depends what it was.
- Feed hardness of 12 °dH. Stable supply, or drifting up two years running?
- Cartridge changed in March. On volume, or on a guess?
- Machine scaling. Despite correct treatment, or because the blend drifted eighteen months ago?

None of those are answerable from one reading. All of them are trivial with a series.

## What to record

**Once, at specification:**

- Full lab analysis of the source water, dated — including chloride, sulphate, silica and sodium.
- Field titration of the feed: alkalinity, calcium hardness, total chlorine.
- Static and dynamic pressure at the machine inlet.
- Which tap the sample came from, and what feeds it.
- The treatment specified, and the reasoning — particularly if you chose *not* to treat something.

**At every cartridge change:**

- Date, cartridge model, rated capacity **at the measured feed hardness**.
- Meter reading at install, and the calculated due volume.
- Volume flushed.
- Bypass or blend setting.
- Post-flush titration at the machine inlet: alkalinity, hardness, total chlorine.

**At every service visit:**

- Feed and treated titration, at the machine inlet.
- Meter reading.
- Differential pressure across the filter train.
- Static and dynamic inlet pressure.
- On RO: feed and permeate conductivity, for the rejection rate.
- Bypass position confirmed against its mark.
- Anything adjusted, and what it was before.

**Monthly, by bar staff:**

- Meter reading, against the cartridge due volume.
- Overnight meter check for leaks.
- Filter differential pressure.

## Where to keep it

Three places, because each fails differently.

**The tag at the filter head.** The minimum viable log, and the one that survives everything. Cartridge model, install date, meter reading, due volume, bypass setting, last titration result. Anyone who opens the cabinet can see the state of the system without finding a document.

**A sheet in the bar.** Where the monthly readings get written. Laminated or on a clipboard, at the point the reading is taken. A sheet in an office upstairs does not get filled in.

**A digital copy you hold.** Because the manager who commissioned the fit-out will leave, and the folder will be thrown out during a refurbishment. Emailed to a role address rather than a person, and kept in your own records.

## The warranty argument

This is the concrete commercial case, and it is worth making to clients explicitly.

Manufacturers specify water quality limits, and those limits are warranty conditions. Scale damage is plainly visible to anyone who opens a machine, so a claim involving a scaled element or heat exchanger will be examined on water quality grounds.

The defence is a dated series of treated-water test results showing compliance with the manufacturer's own specification, alongside meter readings showing cartridges were changed within their rated volume.

Without that record, the client is asserting that the water was fine. With it, they are demonstrating it. On a heat exchanger or a boiler, that difference is the whole value of the machine's remaining life.

> Put the manufacturer's water specification page in the file next to your treated-water results. Those two documents together are the warranty defence, and neither works without the other.

## Reading the trends

What to look for at the annual review:

| Pattern | Means |
| --- | --- |
| Treated alkalinity rising, feed stable | Cartridge exhausting, or bypass drifted |
| Feed alkalinity rising year on year | Genuine supply change — revisit the specification |
| Chloride appearing in winter readings | Road salt; consider RO if it nears threshold |
| RO permeate conductivity rising | Membrane declining; plan replacement |
| Filter differential rising faster each cycle | Sediment loading increasing upstream |
| Consumption per drink rising | Something wasting water — passing valve, longer flushes |
| Everything flat for three years | Genuinely stable; interval can relax slightly |

That last row is worth including deliberately. A log that lets you *reduce* a client's service burden with evidence is as valuable as one that catches a fault.

## Review it annually

Put the log next to the actual fault history and ask two questions. What went wrong that the log should have predicted? And what have we measured repeatedly that has never told us anything?

The first tightens an interval or adds a measurement. The second lets something go. After two years the log should look meaningfully different from the generic version above, and it will be worth considerably more.
